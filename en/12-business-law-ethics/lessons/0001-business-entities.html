Business Entities

The first task in business is not finding customers — it is answering one question: in what legal identity will this business exist? Your legal form decides who answers for debts, where money can come from, whether equity can move, and how you are taxed. It is the first, and the most overlooked, “default contract” a founder ever signs. In this lesson you personally assign an identity to three startup scenarios.

Read this first This lesson is a teaching illustration and does not constitute legal advice. Registration requirements and tax treatment for each form change as statutes are amended; for real decisions, consult a licensed attorney and a tax professional.

1Choosing a form is choosing a whole package of default rules

Many people treat “what to register as” as just another checkbox on a form. In fact, your legal form (business entity) is an entire package of default rules: whose doorstep debts can reach, how profits are split, whether outsiders can invest, whether transferring shares needs anyone’s blessing, and how the tax authorities treat you — every one of these is preset by the form you choose. Whatever your agreements do not spell out, the law fills in for you with defaults; and the defaults may not be what you wanted.

The four mainstream forms line up on a spectrum. At the left end sits the sole proprietorship (in China, the individually-owned business) — easiest to set up, with unlimited liability. At the right end sits the joint-stock company (the corporation) — the heaviest structure and the strongest fundraising power. Between them come the partnership and the limited liability company (LLC). Moving right, liability insulation and fundraising capacity grow — and so do setup costs and the compliance burden. There is no such thing as free liability insulation.

Definition · Legal personality and limited liability

A legal person is a legally constructed “person”: in its own name the company owns property, signs contracts, and sues or gets sued — separate and independent from the founder’s personal assets. From this flows the most important concept of the lesson:

Limited liability: if the company owes money, the owners (limited partners or shareholders, depending on the form) are, as a matter of principle, liable only up to the capital they subscribed; personal homes and savings sit outside the creditor’s reach. By contrast, sole proprietors and general partners bear unlimited liability for business debts, and general partners bear joint and several liability as well — a creditor can demand the entire debt from any single one of them.

Limited liability is not a failsafe firewall: where company assets are misappropriated, personal and company accounts commingled, or subscribed capital siphoned out, courts may “pierce the corporate veil” (disregard of legal personality) and hold the owners personally liable. Limited liability protects the shareholder who plays by the rules — not the one who treats the company as a personal wallet.

Case: Why tech startups almost always use an LLC plus a limited-partnership holding platform

Look at any technology company that has taken institutional investment (illustrative; no specific company named): the fundraising vehicle is almost invariably a limited liability company — because what investors want is calculable risk: put in a sum, own a percentage, lose at most that sum, and never be personally chased for more. The same legal shell holds capital increases, employee option pools, and the governance terms investors require.

The second layer is the more interesting one: employee equity incentives are usually not granted to employees directly, but parked inside a limited partnership — employees join as limited partners (LPs) holding interests and sharing the upside, while the general partner (GP) is the founder or a company the founder controls. Voting power concentrates in the GP’s hands: dozens of employees never enter the shareholder register, shareholders’ meetings do not need every signature, and control is not diluted by the incentive plan; employees, capped at their capital, keep a purely economic upside. One structure solves two problems: the LLC gives investors the confidence to invest, the limited partnership preserves the ability to govern.

What to notice in this case: choosing a form is not paperwork — it is designing the structure that allocates responsibility and power. Who bears risk, who collects returns, and who decides can be arranged separately, in different forms at different layers. This is the founder’s first true exercise in top-level design.

Key Terms

Legal person
A legally constructed independent entity that owns property, concludes contracts, and bears debts in its own name, separate from its investors’ personal assets.
Limited liability
Shareholders are liable for company debts only up to the capital they subscribed; personal assets are, as a rule, beyond creditors’ reach.
Joint and several liability
General partners are collectively liable for partnership debts; a creditor may claim the entire debt from any single partner.
General partnership
A partnership in which all partners bear unlimited joint and several liability, with roles and splits governed by the partnership agreement.
Limited partnership
A form in which general partners (GPs) run the business with unlimited liability and limited partners (LPs) are liable only up to their capital; commonly used as a shareholding platform.
Piercing the corporate veil
When owners abuse the company’s independent status and commingle personal and company affairs, courts may “pierce the veil” and hold them liable for the company’s debts.
Sole proprietorship
The registration form for a business run by an individual or family (China’s individually-owned business): simplest to set up, with the owner bearing unlimited liability.
Key Takeaways

Choosing a form is choosing your default rules: liability, financing, transferability, and taxes come as one bundle. Limited liability protects the rule-abiding investor; fundraising power comes at the price of governance and compliance.

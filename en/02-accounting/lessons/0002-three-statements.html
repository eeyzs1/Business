The Three Financial Statements: A Company’s Report Card

Every year a company hands in a “report card” — but the card is really three statements, each answering a different question: Did we earn anything over this stretch of time? How solid is the family fortune at this moment? How much cash actually came in and went out? The heart of this lesson is seeing how the three statements interlock — and why “made money” does not equal “received money.”

1Three Statements, Three Views of Time

Think of the three statements as three camera lenses, and the confusion clears:

One metaphor to remember all three statements

The income statement is a video — it records the performance of receipts and payments over a period (a month, a quarter, a year); the balance sheet is a photograph — it freezes the family fortune at one instant (December 31, say); the statement of cash flows is a water flow — it tracks every drop of real money that passes through the pipe.

The video tells you whether operations performed well, the photograph tells you how substantial the base is, and the water flow tells you whether the company might die of thirst. Together, the three make one complete report card.

Inside the statement of cash flows are three sections: operating activities (the core business’s receipts and payments), investing activities (buying and building assets), and financing activities (borrowing and repaying). A healthy, growing company usually wants its operating section to be net-positive on its own — able to live without a transfusion.

2Articulation: How the Three Statements Interlock

The three statements are not three isolated islands; fixed corridors run between them, and accounting calls these articulation links. The most important one:

Ending Retained Earnings = Beginning Retained Earnings + Net Income − Dividends to Shareholders

In other words, the net income computed on the income statement flows, at period-end, through the retained earnings account into owners’ equity on the balance sheet — that is exactly how the lemonade stand’s ¥90 profit rolled into equity in the last lesson. The other corridor sits in the statement of cash flows: its ending cash balance must equal the cash reported on the balance sheet. The two statements act like a pair of clasps, holding the income statement between them.

Profit ≠ Cash The income statement is kept on the accrual basis: revenue is recognized when earned, not when the money arrives. A credit sale lifts profit while cash stands still; depreciation works the other way, cutting profit while not a single coin leaves. The crack runs in both directions, and the statement of cash flows is what patches it.

What the three routes share: no transaction touches only one statement. A credit sale rewrites the income statement and the balance sheet at once; depreciation rewrites the income statement and the net book value of fixed assets; a repayment rewrites the statement of cash flows and both sides of the balance sheet. The three statements are one linked system, not three independent files.

4Case Study: A Milk-Tea Shop’s Monthly Three Statements

Simplified three statements for “First Snow Tea,” month four after opening (figures are illustrative)

This little shop lost a cumulative ¥16,000 over its first three months (weighed down by fit-out and opening promotions); this month it turns its first profit. The three statements record the month together like this:

Income statement (this month)AmountStatement of cash flows (this month)Amount
Revenue (incl. ¥10,000 of credit sales to a business client)¥120,000Operating: cash collected from sales (¥120,000 − ¥10,000 credit)+¥110,000
Cost of materials¥48,000Operating: payments for materials, labor, and rent−¥84,000
Labor and rent¥36,000Net cash from operations+¥26,000
Depreciation (ice machine, etc.)¥6,000Financing: repayment of opening-loan principal−¥10,000
Net income (25% net margin)¥30,000Net increase in cash (¥20,000 at start → ¥36,000 at end)+¥16,000

Month-end balance sheet: cash ¥36,000 + accounts receivable ¥10,000 + materials inventory ¥8,000 + equipment, net ¥54,000 (cost ¥60,000 − accumulated depreciation ¥6,000) = assets ¥108,000; liabilities = bank loan ¥50,000 (¥60,000 − ¥10,000 repaid this month) + accounts payable ¥4,000 = ¥54,000; equity = paid-in capital ¥40,000 + retained earnings ¥14,000 (last month −¥16,000 + this month’s net income ¥30,000) = ¥54,000. ¥108,000 = ¥54,000 + ¥54,000 — the photograph and the video interlock perfectly.

What this case teaches: this month’s net income is ¥30,000, yet net operating cash inflow is only ¥26,000 — the ¥4,000 gap = the ¥10,000 tied up in credit sales − the ¥6,000 of depreciation that required no cash. Profit and cash are two different rulers: use only one of them, and the other will ambush the founder.

Key Terms

Income Statement
The “video” that records revenue, expenses, and profit over a period.
Balance Sheet
The “photograph” that freezes assets, liabilities, and equity at one point in time.
Statement of Cash Flows
The “water flow” that tracks cash in and out across operating, investing, and financing.
Articulation
The fixed, checkable quantity corridors between statements, such as net income flowing into retained earnings.
Net Income
The final profit after all expenses and taxes are deducted from revenue.
Retained Earnings
Profits accumulated over the years and not distributed to shareholders; a component of equity.
Accrual Basis
The bookkeeping basis that recognizes revenue and expenses when they occur, not when cash changes hands.
Depreciation
The expense that spreads the cost of long-lived assets such as equipment over their useful lives.
Key Takeaways
The income statement is a video, the balance sheet a photograph, the statement of cash flows a water flow; net income flows into the balance sheet through retained earnings, and ending cash must agree with the balance sheet. The three move as one — and profit ≠ cash.

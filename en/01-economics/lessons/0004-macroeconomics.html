Economics · Lesson 4

Macroeconomics: GDP, Inflation, and Interest Rates

The first three lessons looked at one good and one firm; this one zooms all the way out to an entire country: how much was produced in a year (GDP), how fast money is losing its value (inflation), how many people are out of work (unemployment) — and what happens after the central bank presses the ‘rate hike’ button. That chain reaction is the front-page logic of financial news.

1GDP: taking stock of a year’s output

Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the market value of all final goods and services an economy produces over a period. Two keywords: ‘final’ — intermediate goods are excluded to avoid double counting (the flour inside the bread is counted only once); ‘domestic’ — anything produced within the country counts, whoever owns the factory. The most common measurement is the expenditure approach: add up everything society spends, in four categories.

GDP = C + I + G + NX

Consumption (C) is household spending; investment (I) is firms buying equipment, building plants, and adding inventory (note: buying shares is not investment — it merely changes the asset’s owner); government purchases (G) is what government pays for output; net exports (NX) = exports − imports. The four blocks rise and fall against one another and add up to total output.

Common miscounts

① The full price of a second-hand home: an asset transfer, not new output (the agent’s service fee does count). ② Unemployment benefits and other transfer payments: no corresponding output, so they are not in G. ③ Buying and selling shares: a financial transaction, not investment I. In all three cases ‘money moved’, but GDP doesn’t budge.

2Inflation and unemployment: the economy’s thermometer

The consumer price index (CPI) tracks the average price of a basket of consumer goods and services; a year-on-year rise in the CPI is inflation. The causes fall roughly into three types: demand-pull (too much money chasing goods), cost-push (raw materials and wages getting pricier), and excessive money creation. Inflation erodes purchasing power — it is ‘friction on money’. The unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force (employed + actively looking); people who have left the labor force are not in the denominator. In the short run, inflation and unemployment often sit on a seesaw (the Phillips curve, illustrative), which keeps policymakers forever torn.

3Interest rates: the economy’s master valve

The whole economy is an interlocking web of money flows: the circular flow diagram links households, firms, government, banks (finance), and the rest of the world into a closed loop — your spending is someone else’s income, and someone else’s investment is tomorrow’s supply. The central bank’s policy rate is the master valve on all borrowing in this web.

Simulator · Press the rate-hike button: the transmission chain, step by step
  1. The central bank raises the policy rate (e.g. the federal funds rate) — the ‘wholesale price’ of money gets dearer.
  2. Commercial banks raise deposit and loan rates in turn — the ‘retail price’ faced by firms and households gets dearer.
  3. Willingness to borrow falls: firms take on less debt to expand (I↓); households take fewer mortgages and car loans and run up less credit (C↓).
  4. Aggregate demand cools: goods and services stop flying off the shelves, firms lose the urge to raise prices, and expansion and hiring turn cautious.
  5. Inflation pressure eases — typically with a lag of six months to over a year before fully showing (illustrative), and the price is usually slower growth and employment.

4Case: the 2022–2023 global inflation and rate-hike cycle

The Fed steps on the brakes

From 2021, the post-pandemic demand rebound, snarled supply chains, and energy and food price shocks piled up, and global inflation soared: US CPI inflation reached about 9% year-on-year in mid-2022 (roughly a forty-year high, illustrative magnitude). The Federal Reserve’s response was the textbook transmission chain: from March 2022 it hiked rates more than ten times in a row, lifting the federal funds rate from about 0–0.25% to about 5.25–5.5% (held after July 2023, illustrative magnitude). Mortgage, auto-loan, and credit-card rates rose in step; housing and durable-goods spending cooled visibly; by 2023 inflation had fallen back to roughly the 3–4% range (illustrative magnitude) — at the cost of persistent market worry about recession and jobs.

The lesson: monetary policy fights inflation through exactly one mechanism — raising the price of money to cool aggregate demand; it works slowly and with lags, and almost always at a cost to growth and jobs. When the financial news says ‘a 25-basis-point hike’, put it back on the transmission chain and it stops being jargon.

Key Terms

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)
The market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period.
Expenditure Approach
Measuring GDP by summing the four spending categories C+I+G+NX.
Consumer Price Index (CPI)
An index of the average price change for a basket of consumer goods and services.
Inflation
A sustained rise in the overall price level; money’s purchasing power falls.
Unemployment Rate
The share of the labor force (employed plus job-seekers) that is unemployed.
Policy Rate
The benchmark rate a central bank uses to steer commercial banks’ cost of funds.
Circular Flow Diagram
A macro schematic of spending and income circulating among sectors.
Key Takeaways

The macroeconomy is an interlocking web: your spending is someone else’s income (GDP = C+I+G+NX), and the central bank’s rate is the master valve on all borrowing. A rate hike = braking: it cools aggregate demand, tames inflation, and pays for it with growth.

NPV: Deciding Whether a Project Is Worth It

Before you sits a project: open a store, buy equipment, launch a new system. Invest, or not? Lesson 1’s time value and Lesson 2’s risk premium converge here into a single yardstick — net present value — turning “a debate of gut feelings” into “one arithmetic problem.”

1The Yardstick: Project Return vs. the Cost of Capital

Comparing a project’s total outlay with its total income is wrong — money at different points in time cannot be added directly. The right question is always: after every cash flow is discounted to the same point in time, can the project beat the return from putting the money somewhere else? That “return somewhere else” is the cost of capital, the discount rate of the previous lesson: the risk-free rate plus a premium for the project’s risk. Steady, defensible cash flows can be discounted at a lower rate; an unproven new product must carry a higher one.

2Discounted Cash Flow and Net Present Value

Discounted cash flow (DCF): divide each future year’s cash flow back to today by (1+r)t, then subtract the initial outlay, and you get the net present value (NPV):

NPV = −I + CF1/(1+r) + CF2/(1+r)2 + … + CFn/(1+r)n
One rule, and only one

NPV > 0: the project earns more than the cost of capital and creates value for the firm — invest. NPV < 0: it fails to earn even the baseline return the capital should earn — it destroys value; walk away. NPV = 0: it exactly matches the baseline; invest or not, you are indifferent. You can read NPV as “how much richer you are after correcting for time and risk.”

3IRR: Asking the Question a Different Way

The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero — intuitively, the project’s own compound rate of return. Using it is just as simple: invest if IRR is above the cost of capital, pass if it is below — usually the same verdict NPV gives. Know its limits too: IRR is a percentage and says nothing about scale (a small project returning 30% can carry a smaller NPV than a large one returning 12%); and when the cash-flow sign flips back and forth, there can even be multiple IRRs. When in doubt, let NPV rule.

4Two Classic Traps: Sunk Cost and Opportunity Cost

Trap 1: a sunk cost is not a cost

A sunk cost is money already spent and unrecoverable. The ¥200,000 sunk into last year’s renovation is gone whether or not you invest today, so it is irrelevant to the decision. Decide only on the incremental cash flows from this point forward.

Trap 2: an opportunity cost is a cost

The reverse trap: a project that never sends you a bill can still be expensive. Opening a store in your own building pays no rent, but it gives up the rent the space could collect — that opportunity cost must enter the cash flows (valued at the best forgone use). Your own funds are no different: investing them means giving up the return of lending them out.

5Case: Is That Coffee Shop Worth Opening?

The arithmetic behind one latte

Suppose you take over a small shop (all figures illustrative): an initial outlay of about ¥600,000 (renovation, equipment, and the transfer deposit); expected monthly revenue around ¥80,000, assuming cash retained after all operating payments is about 15% of revenue — ¥144,000 a year, rounded to ¥140,000 in the simulator (working-capital changes and terminal recoveries are omitted here; net profit and cash flow are different); an 8-year horizon; and, given your own read of the risks, a required return of 8%. Dial those parameters into the decision machine above and you get: a static payback of a little over four years, an NPV of about +¥200,000, and an IRR above 8% — if the assumptions hold, this shop is worth opening. Watch the sensitive spot: let retained cash as a share of revenue fall from 15% to 10% and NPV can turn negative. A project’s life or death often hangs on a single assumption.

The lesson: the counterexample is a classic too: in 2008, having over-expanded straight into a financial crisis, Starbucks announced it would close about 600 underperforming US stores. However painful the renovation costs, they are sunk — the decision looks only at those stores’ future cash flows: negative NPV, so close them.

Key Terms

Discounted cash flow (DCF)
A valuation method that discounts each future cash flow to present value and adds them up.
Net present value (NPV)
The sum of the present values of future cash flows, minus the initial outlay.
Cost of capital
The required return on money invested in this project; used as the discount rate.
Internal rate of return (IRR)
The discount rate at which NPV is exactly zero; the project’s intrinsic return.
Sunk cost
Spending already incurred and unrecoverable; irrelevant to the current decision.
Opportunity cost
The return on the best alternative forgone by choosing this project.
Payback period
The years needed to recoup the initial outlay, with no discounting.
Key Takeaways

Discount every cash flow back to today and add them up: that is NPV — above zero it creates value, below zero it destroys it, and when in doubt NPV rules. Sunk costs are irrelevant to the decision; opportunity costs must be counted.

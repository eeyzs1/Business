Strategy · Lesson 4

The Business Model Canvas

“Getting great content in front of people” can be three completely different businesses: selling discs, running cinemas, or streaming by subscription. Strategic positioning answers “why do you win”; the business model answers how value turns into cash — and how cash turns back into value. This lesson teaches you to take any business apart with the nine-block, one-page Business Model Canvas — including your own.

1Nine Blocks: A Whole Business on One Page

Proposed by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur in Business Model Generation, the canvas breaks any business model into nine building blocks:

AreaBuilding blocksOne question to ask yourself
Customer sideCustomer Segments, Channels, Customer RelationshipsWhom do we serve? How do we reach them? How do we keep them?
Value coreValue PropositionWhat job do we help with, what pain do we relieve?
Efficiency sideKey Activities, Key Resources, Key PartnersWhich people, activities, and assets deliver the promise?
The moneyRevenue Streams, Cost StructureWho pays, and how? What burns the most cash?
Concept Card · Business Model

The fundamental logic of how a firm creates value, delivers value, and captures value. It is not strategy: strategy chooses which battle to fight, while the business model is that battle’s logistics system — where the grain comes from, how the wounded are treated, how the captured towns are taxed.

2Efficiency on the Left, Value on the Right: The Physics of the Canvas

Fold the canvas down the middle and a clear dividing line appears. The right wing (value side) — customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams — answers “what value for whom, how is it delivered, how is it charged”; the left wing (efficiency side) — key partners, key activities, key resources, cost structure — answers “what infrastructure produces the whole thing efficiently.”

The division of labor between the wings sets a business’s ceiling and floor: the right wing fixes the revenue ceiling (how much customers will pay for a unit of value), the left wing fixes the cost floor (the least it can cost to deliver that unit). Profit lives between the two lines. A dazzling right wing on a collapsing left wing loses money on every sale; a lean left wing without a standing right wing is a money-saving machine nobody visits.

3Telling the Story with the Canvas: Four Steps Through a Business

  1. For whom: start by circling customer segments — not “everyone,” but specific people whose faces you can picture.
  2. Offering what: the value proposition — which jobs you help finish, which pains you ease, which gains you create.
  3. Delivered how: channels and customer relationships — where they hear about you, where they buy, and why they stay afterward.
  4. Earning how: revenue streams and cost structure — who pays, how often, and which block eats most of the money.

This is the right narrative order for introducing a business to investors or new hires — right to left, then down to the money at the bottom. Reverse it (opening with how hard you work and how impressive your assets are) and the audience usually feels nothing.

Case · Netflix: One Canvas, Redrawn Three Times

  • circa 1997–2000 · DVD-by-mail subscription: the customers were movie fans fed up with video-store late fees; the value proposition was “unlimited rentals for a monthly fee, no late fees”; the channel was the mailbox; revenue was a monthly subscription. Note that the disruption was written into the “Revenue Streams” block from day one — not into the discs themselves.
  • circa 2007 · streaming launches: the channel shifted from the post office to the internet and device apps, and marginal delivery cost collapsed; the customer relationship went from “waiting for the disc” to “the recommendation algorithm is the relationship”; the cost structure tilted from logistics and warehouses to bandwidth and content licensing.
  • circa 2013 to today · original content: the key resource swapped licensed rights for a self-produced, exclusive content library, and key partners became production companies and creators; revenue stayed subscription, but the value proposition upgraded to “shows you can only find here” — the moat (last lesson) got poured into the “Key Resources” block.

The lesson of this case: none of Netflix’s leaps was a single-block optimization — each was a whole-canvas redraw of channels, revenue, resources, and costs together (the years and descriptions above are illustrative readings of public information). A business model is not strategy; but without a model that actually collects money, even the best strategy remains a manifesto.

Key Terms

Business Model
The fundamental logic by which a firm creates, delivers, and captures value — how value turns into cash.
Business Model Canvas
A one-page, nine-block tool that breaks any business model into building blocks you can discuss and redraw.
Value Proposition
The bundle of jobs done, pains relieved, and gains created for a customer.
Customer Segments
The people or organizations a firm chooses to serve; “everyone” means no segmentation at all.
Channels
Every touchpoint through which customers learn about, evaluate, buy, and receive the value.
Revenue Streams
The cash a firm collects from each customer segment, one-off or recurring.
Recurring Revenue
Revenue that flows in continuously through subscriptions or renewals; the lifeblood of subscription models.
Cost Structure
All the costs of running the business model, and their fixed-versus-variable mix.
Key Takeaways

Nine blocks: the right wing sets the ceiling on value and revenue, the left wing sets the floor on cost and efficiency, and profit lives between the two wings. Tell the story as “for whom → offering what → delivered how → earning how”; a business leaps by redrawing the whole canvas, not by patching a single block.

The Time Value of Money

The same ¥100 is not worth the same when you get it today versus next year. This seemingly simple intuition is the foundation of the entire edifice of finance: loan rates, insurance pricing, company valuation, retirement planning — all of them are answering one question: what is future money worth today?

1Why ¥100 Today Is Worth More

Money sitting at different points in time cannot be added directly, for three reasons:

Three reasons

Opportunity cost. Money in hand today can be put to work at once — invested in production or lent out at interest — while money you wait for misses a year of returns. The return you give up when you lend money out is the true price of funds.

Inflation. In most years prices drift gently upward, so the same banknote usually buys less next year than it does this year.

Risk. Future promises may not be kept — the borrower defaults, the company fails, policy changes. Waiting itself exposes you to uncertainty.

So “time” itself has a price, and that price is the interest rate. Accepting this is the first step of every financial calculation.

2Future Value and Compounding: The Snowball Rolls

Push today’s money forward and you get its future value (FV); today’s amount itself is the present value (PV). There is only one core formula:

FV = PV × (1 + r)n

Here r is the interest rate per period and n is the number of periods. Simple interest pays interest on the principal alone; compound interest means “interest earning interest” — each period’s interest joins the principal, and the next period is charged on a larger base. For the first few years the two barely differ; later the gap tears open: the snowball’s payoff lives in the back half. Seen in reverse, it is just as brutal: the higher the rate and the longer the term, the harder the formula’s exponential effect hits — which is exactly why high-rate online loans and credit-card installments hurt so much: the snowball can roll backward too, and then it crushes the borrower.

3Discounting: Pulling the Future Back to Today

Discounting is the reverse of compounding — divide future money back and convert it into today’s present value:

PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n

Here r is also called the discount rate — the return you “require”: it compensates for the opportunity cost, inflation, and risk above. Example: ¥1,050,000 certain to come back in one year, with the firm requiring a 5% annual return, is worth 1,050,000 ÷ 1.05 = ¥1,000,000 today. If that IOU sells for only ¥980,000 right now, buying it nets ¥20,000 of present value — exactly the seed of Lesson 3’s NPV.

4The Rule of 72: Doubling in Your Head

Mental-math tool

The Rule of 72: at an annual rate of r%, money takes roughly 72 ÷ r years to double. At 8% a year, about 9 years; at 3%, about 24; at 12%, about 6. It is an approximation (the exact answer is ln2 ÷ ln(1+r)) and is most accurate in the 6%–10% range, but it is plenty for sanity-checking the numbers in financial ads.

5Case: Start Ten Years Earlier, End Up a Million Ahead

Chen at 25 vs. Wang at 35

Both plan to retire at 60 and invest ¥1,000 a month into the same broad-market index fund, assumed to return about 7% a year over the long run (illustrative assumption: the historical magnitude of long-run stock market returns, not a forecast; in practice there will be volatile years and losing years).

  • Chen starts at 25: 420 monthly contributions, ¥420,000 of principal in total, and about ¥1,800,000 at age 60;
  • Wang starts at 35: 300 contributions, ¥300,000 of principal, and about ¥810,000.

Wang invests only ¥120,000 less principal, yet ends up about ¥1,000,000 behind — and the bulk of the gap is not “extra money saved” but the earliest batches of money rolling through ten extra years of compounding.

Illustrative ending values: Chen ¥0 · Wang ¥0 (monthly-compounding annuity formula)

The lesson: the time value of money is not an abstract formula; it is the mathematical case for starting early. Run the same formula in reverse and you have the machinery for mortgage payments and retirement shortfalls — the numbers will make the argument for you.

Key Terms

Present value (PV)
The value today of a sum of money received in the future.
Future value (FV)
The value that today’s money compounds to by a future date.
Compound interest
Interest that joins the principal and then earns interest itself.
Simple interest
Interest charged on the original principal only.
Discounting
The reverse operation that converts future cash flows into present value.
Discount rate (r)
The “required return” used in that conversion; compensates opportunity cost, inflation, and risk.
Rule of 72
Divide 72 by the annual interest-rate percentage for a quick estimate of the years to double.
Opportunity cost
The return on the best alternative forgone by making a choice.
Key Takeaways

Money today is worth more than money tomorrow; opportunity cost, inflation, and risk are the three reasons. Compounding makes time an ally — the earlier you start, the bigger the snowball. Any future amount must first be discounted before it can be compared with money today.

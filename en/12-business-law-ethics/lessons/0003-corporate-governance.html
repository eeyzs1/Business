Corporate Governance: The Agency Problem

The modern company is born with a crack in it: the people who supply the money do not run the business, and the people who run the business did not supply the money. Corporate governance is the institutional design built around that crack — making the people who manage your money afraid to stray, and willing to do the job well. Once you understand it, you understand what the news buzzwords — boards of directors, independent directors, information disclosure — are actually guarding against.

Read this first The governance organs described in this lesson are a teaching illustration, not legal advice: statutory organs and their powers differ across countries, regions, and corporate forms — the applicable company law and the company’s own articles of association prevail.

1Separation breeds the problem: principal and agent

In a small noodle shop the owner cooks personally — nobody worries on anyone else’s behalf. But as a company grows and goes public, with shareholders scattered into the thousands, the separation of ownership and control sets in: shareholders put up the money and keep the residual returns, while management runs daily operations. Economics calls this the principal–agent relationship: shareholders are the principals, managers are their agents.

The crack opens in two places. First, information asymmetry: managers know the company’s true condition better than anyone — whether inventory is piling up, whether collections are strained, where the money is going. Second, moral hazard: in the corners no one watches, agents may slack, splurge, or chase personal glory — palatial offices, dubious acquisitions, a final sprint of short-term numbers before retirement. Note: most managers are not out to do harm, but when institutions leave the door unguarded, even good people drift into the gray zone.

Definition · Agency cost

In their classic paper, Jensen and Meckling called the principal’s full cost of “taming” the agent the agency cost. It comes roughly in three parts:

① Monitoring costs — building and running audits, boards, and disclosure systems; ② bonding costs — the agent’s cost of proving its good faith, such as layer after layer of approvals and compliance procedures; ③ residual loss — the value still destroyed by misaligned interests despite all that. The whole point of corporate governance is to keep the sum of these three costs below the agency benefits received in exchange (the value of professional management).

2The governance toolbox: caging power with process

Since self-discipline cannot be assumed for everyone, “hard to do wrong” gets designed into the structure itself. The mainstream governance tools, sorted by who checks whom:

Do not forget the second front: protecting minority shareholders. Between controlling and minority shareholders runs the same kind of agency conflict — cases of “tunneling” value out of listed companies through related-party transactions and fund appropriation surface in enforcement disclosures constantly (illustrative). Institutional responses include cumulative voting, recusal of interested shareholders from votes, independent directors’ opinions, regulatory inquiries, and litigation remedies (illustrative). In one sentence: governance must guard not only against “lazy managers” but also against “the controlling shareholder’s reaching hand.”

Case: Berkshire Hathaway’s boardroom culture — hedging process with trust (illustrative)

Berkshire Hathaway offers an unconventional governance specimen. Public records show that the holding company’s headquarters has long employed only about two dozen people (approximate) while overseeing dozens of subsidiaries; Warren Buffett himself stresses, in letter after letter to shareholders, that headquarters sets almost no operating procedures for the subsidiaries and hands business decisions back to their managers.

How does it keep agency costs down? First, deeply bound interests: the bulk of the Buffett family’s wealth is Berkshire stock — the helmsman is himself the largest “principal,” eating his own cooking and bearing his own risk. Second, the board has long consisted of people who know the businesses and have skin in the game, and its meetings deal in substance rather than ceremony (illustrative). Third, pick the right people, then let go: subsidiary managers are mostly industry veterans, trust and delegation replace thick approval manuals, and tenure runs long.

But note the precondition: “light on process, heavy on trust” works only because the ultimate accountable party (Buffett) is himself trustworthy, expert, and interest-aligned — the vast majority of listed companies with dispersed ownership cannot replicate that kind of personal trust, and must return to “institutional safeguards first”: a board, independent directors, audit and disclosure — none of them optional.

What to notice in this case: the goal of governance is not the thickest possible rulebook but aligned incentives and trust — what alignment can solve, do not try to plug with process; process exists to guard against the part of human nature that alignment cannot reach.

Key Terms

Principal–agent problem
When the principal’s (shareholders’) goals diverge from the agent’s (managers’) and information is asymmetric, the agent may act against the principal’s interests.
Information asymmetry
One party to a transaction or agency relationship holds more of the key information than the other.
Moral hazard
In unwatched corners, the agent behaves in ways that hurt the principal — shirking, extravagance, excessive risk-taking.
Agency cost
The sum of monitoring costs, bonding costs, and residual loss, all born of misaligned interests (Jensen and Meckling).
Independent director
A director with no company post and no material interest in management, giving independent opinions on matters such as related-party transactions.
Supervisory board
A dedicated oversight organ: inspects the company’s finances and monitors how directors and senior managers perform.
Information disclosure
The system of periodic reports and major-event announcements that uses transparency to shrink information asymmetry.
Related-party transaction
Transactions between the company and its related parties — the classic channel for value transfer and “tunneling.”
Key Takeaways

The separation of ownership and control inevitably breeds agency problems: information asymmetry supplies the opportunity, misaligned goals the motive. Every design of governance — the organs, disclosure, incentives — exists for one purpose: that the people who manage your money dare not stray, and want to do the job well.

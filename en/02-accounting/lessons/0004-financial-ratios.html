Financial Ratios: A Company Checkup

Is “net income of ¥5 billion” good news? It depends — whether it sits on ¥20 billion or ¥500 billion of assets changes the answer completely. A ratio turns an absolute number into a comparable one, taking the company’s blood pressure and lipid panel the way a checkup does. And the finale is the DuPont analysis: one multiplication that splits shareholder return into three utterly different ways of making money.

1Why Ratios: Absolute Numbers Have No Context

The idea behind financial ratios is simple: divide one statement number by another, and the effect of size cancels out. A company with ¥100 billion of revenue and one with ¥1 billion cannot be compared directly, but “how much net profit each ¥1 of assets squeezes out in a year” is comparable for anyone. The common ratios fall into three families, by checkup item:

Checkup itemRatioFormulaWhat it asks
Solvency · blood pressureCurrent RatioCurrent assets ÷ current liabilitiesCan debts due within a year be paid?
Debt-to-AssetsTotal liabilities ÷ total assetsWhat share of the asset base is borrowed?
Profitability · blood lipidsGross MarginGross profit ÷ revenueIs the product itself worth money?
Net Profit MarginNet income ÷ revenueOf every ¥1 sold, how much stays in the pocket?
Return on Equity (ROE)Net income ÷ shareholders’ equityHow much does each ¥1 shareholders invest earn back?
Efficiency · heart rateInventory TurnoverCost of goods sold ÷ average inventoryHow many times a year does shelf capital turn?
Receivables TurnoverRevenue ÷ average receivablesHow fast do credit sales come home as cash?
The golden rule of ratios A ratio only means something within the same industry, the same period, and the same definitions: a current ratio of 0.9 can be normal for a supermarket (cash comes back almost instantly) and an alarm bell for an asset-heavy property developer. Ask “compared with whom?” before judging any number high or low.

2The DuPont Analysis: A Multiplication Map

The most celebrated synthesis of the three families is the DuPont analysis — it splits return on equity (ROE) into a product of three factors:

ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier

The three factors stand for three ways of making money: what price you charge (profit per ¥1 of revenue), how fast you turn (revenue per ¥1 of assets in a year), and how much you borrow (the inverse of equity’s share of assets). The elegance: three different roads all reach the same destination, so two companies with identical ROE can run entirely opposite business models — which is precisely the answer to the last lesson’s “90% vs. 20% gross margin” puzzle.

4Case Study: Two Very Different Roads to a High ROE

Walmart-style turnover vs. Moutai-style margin (orders of magnitude, illustrative)

Run the DuPont lens over two classic businesses (all numbers approximate and illustrative): the budget grocer (Walmart is the benchmark) earns a paper-thin net margin of roughly 2%–3%; but its shelves turn over about two to three times a year, and with modest leverage its ROE holds in the double digits for years on end. The premium baijiu maker (Kweichow Moutai is the benchmark) nets over 30%, while its inventory must rest in cellars for years — turnover is glacial — yet it barely needs to borrow, and its ROE is likewise respectably high year after year.

The contrast is vivid: one multiplies a 2% margin by lightning turnover, the other multiplies a 30% margin by leisurely turnover — two entirely different roads, both leading to respectable shareholder returns. The third road is leverage: raise the equity multiplier and ROE instantly “looks good,” but what the income statement amplifies at the same time is the interest burden — and when the tide goes out, the most leveraged company runs aground first.

What this case teaches: before applauding a high ROE, run a DuPont breakdown and ask whether it was earned by margin, turned out by efficiency, or borrowed out by leverage. The three sources carry entirely different risk — and different quality.

Key Terms

Financial Ratio
A comparable metric obtained by dividing two statement figures.
Current Ratio
Current assets ÷ current liabilities; a gauge of short-term solvency.
Debt-to-Assets
Total liabilities ÷ total assets; a gauge of long-run leverage.
Net Profit Margin
Net income ÷ revenue; what lands in the pocket per yuan of sales.
Asset Turnover
Revenue ÷ average total assets; how many times the assets turn in a year.
Equity Multiplier
Total assets ÷ shareholders’ equity; the yardstick of leverage.
DuPont Analysis
The framework that splits ROE into net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier.
Return on Equity (ROE)
Net income ÷ shareholders’ equity; the annual return on each yuan shareholders invest.
Key Takeaways
Ratios turn absolute numbers into comparable ones — read them in three families: solvency, profitability, efficiency. The DuPont analysis tells us ROE = net margin × turnover × leverage. Thin margin with high volume and fat margin with slow volume can both win; when you see a high ROE, ask where it came from — above all when the answer is leverage.

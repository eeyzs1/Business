Elasticity: Why Raising Prices Doesn’t Always Raise Revenue

Farmers pray for a bumper harvest, yet economics tells you: in a bumper year, farmers’ income can actually shrink. The same price increase makes some industries rich and prices others out of existence. The difference comes down to one word: elasticity — how sensitive your customers are when you raise the price.

1‘Raise prices or not’ is an existential question for every business

Pricing is the most direct lever in business: nudge the price up and gross margin thickens instantly — who wouldn’t want that? But the law of demand from Lesson 1 already warned you: raise the price and volume falls. So the real question becomes: what happens to revenue? Do you gain more from the higher unit price, or lose more from the volume gone? The answer depends on whether customers, facing a higher price, turn around and leave, or keep buying exactly as before. Economists named this sensitivity the price elasticity of demand.

2What elasticity is: one ratio, three temperaments

Price elasticity of demand E = | % change in quantity demanded% change in price |

The numerator is how fast customers leave; the denominator is how hard you pushed the price. Take the absolute value and sort into three bands:

|E| > 1 · Elastic

Demand is price-sensitive: raise the price 10% and you lose more than 10% of your customers. Typical of goods with many substitutes, non-essentials, and things people shop around for — bubble tea, airline tickets, a particular brand of shampoo.

|E| < 1 · Inelastic

Demand is price-insensitive: raise the price 10% and fewer than 10% of customers leave. Typical of necessities with few substitutes and no short-term alternatives — staple food, salt, life-saving medicine, the subway you ride every day.

|E| = 1 · Unit Elastic

The two sides exactly offset: the percentage lost in quantity equals the percentage gained in price. This is the watershed for revenue — real markets rarely land on it exactly, but it is the baseline for judgment.

3Elasticity decides revenue: the price-hike seesaw

Total revenue (TR) = price P × quantity sold Q. A price increase is like stepping on a seesaw: the P end rises while the Q end sinks — which way total revenue tips depends on elasticity: in inelastic markets, a price hike raises revenue; in elastic markets, it cuts revenue; at unit elasticity it breaks exactly even. Three questions to judge elasticity

① Are there substitutes? The more substitutes, the more elastic. ② Is it a necessity? The more necessary, the more inelastic. ③ Is it a big share of the budget? The bigger the spend, the more carefully customers weigh it (housing, cars) — and the more elastic it is. One more note: elasticity usually grows with time — the month fuel prices spike, everyone keeps driving; two years later, many have switched to electric cars.

4Case: grain and the iPhone, two fates

The paradox of plenty vs. the brand premium

Grain (inelastic): with kind weather the supply curve shifts right and grain prices collapse. But nobody eats three extra bowls of rice because rice got cheaper — demand for staples is deeply inelastic (researchers commonly use |E| on the order of 0.1–0.3, illustrative). Prices fall 20% while quantity sold rises only a small fraction of that, so total revenue falls: a good harvest hurts farmers — the classic ‘paradox of plenty’. Farmers hold no pricing power and must take the market price passively, so a bumper crop becomes bad news.

iPhone (engineering demand into ‘inelastic’): Apple holds its prices high year after year and discounts older models only modestly, yet sales stay firm — brand loyalty, the iOS ecosystem, and cross-device integration make switching costly, so demand is clearly inelastic: a price rise loses few customers while earning more profit per unit. Public reporting over the years has shown Apple routinely capturing most of the global smartphone industry’s profit (order-of-magnitude, illustrative).

The lesson: the same inelastic demand is a moat for a seller with pricing power (Apple) but a risk for farmers without it (the paradox of plenty) — the difference lies not in elasticity itself, but in how much competition you face and how many substitutes your customers have.

Key Terms

Price Elasticity of Demand
The ratio (in absolute value) of the percentage change in quantity demanded to the percentage change in price; a measure of customers’ price sensitivity.
Elastic
|E| > 1: the percentage change in quantity demanded exceeds the percentage change in price.
Inelastic
|E| < 1: the percentage change in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage change in price.
Unit Elastic
|E| = 1: price and quantity move in equal opposite proportions; revenue is unchanged.
Total Revenue
Price × quantity sold, i.e. P × Q.
Paradox of Plenty
After a supply surge in an inelastic good, the revenue lost to falling prices outweighs the revenue gained from higher volume, so total revenue falls.
Key Takeaways

Whether a price increase makes money is decided not by your courage but by your customers’ alternatives: in elastic markets, price hikes cut revenue; in inelastic markets, they raise it. Before you set a price, ask one question — can my customers walk away?

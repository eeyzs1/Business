Business Law & Ethics · Lesson 4

Business Ethics & ESG

The law tells you what you may not do; ethics asks what you ought to do beyond that. On a company’s true balance sheet sits one asset that is never booked, yet decides its long-run valuation — trust. This lesson teaches you two languages: the frameworks of ethical decision-making, and ESG, which turns “doing good” into manageable metrics. Then you step into two dilemmas with no standard answers and watch, first-hand, how trust gets earned — and how it gets squandered.

1Whom is the company for? Shareholder primacy vs stakeholders

In 1970, the economist Milton Friedman wrote his famous dictum: the one social responsibility of business is to increase profits for shareholders (shareholder primacy) — so long as the firm makes money within the rules, it is already contributing to society. That idea dominated business schools and Wall Street for the following decades.

The challenger is stakeholder theory: a company is a web of relationships — customers want safe products, employees fair treatment, suppliers punctual payment, communities a clean environment — and shareholders are one node in the web, not its only beneficiary. Create value for all stakeholders, and shareholder value is maximized in the long run anyway; put shareholders before everyone else, and the quarterly report may sparkle while trust quietly leaks away.

Definition · Two views of the firm

Shareholder primacy: managers answer to shareholders, profit is the scoreboard, and discipline comes mainly from law and markets.

Stakeholder view: managers answer to everyone the firm touches, trust is the scoreboard, and discipline includes ethics, reputation, and long-term relationships. The two are not a battle of good and evil but a dispute over time horizons: stretch the horizon far enough and they largely converge; a short-sighted “shareholder primacy” and a sentimental “stakeholderism” each drift off course on their own.

2The three gates of ethical decision-making: legality → policy → conscience

For the gray zones, business schools offer a plain, sturdy framework — the three gates. Pass them in order, and skip none:

  • Gate 1 · Legality: Is it illegal? Compliance is the floor, and it is not up for debate. Note: legality answers only “will we be punished?” — never “should we do it?”
  • Gate 2 · Policy: Does it match the values, the code of conduct, and the promises to customers that we ourselves wrote down? Company policy is the lettering you nailed to your own wall.
  • Gate 3 · Conscience: Past the first two gates, one more remains — the newspaper test: if this decision made the front page tomorrow, what would the headline say? Would you want your family to read it?

Most companies that “blow up” do not fail Gate 1 — they fail by pretending Gate 3 does not exist: every single step is “legal,” yet strung together they amount to a catastrophe. The reminder of long-termism: trust behaves like compound interest — slow to build, quick to liquidate. It cannot be bought back with one PR campaign; it can only be accumulated, choice by choice, in the face of temptation.

3The three pillars of ESG and spotting greenwashing

ESG (environmental, social, governance) is the framework that translates ethical issues into measurable indicators — by now the shared language of investors and regulators:

PillarWhat it examinesExample issues (qualitative)
E · EnvironmentalThe operation’s impact on natureCarbon emissions and energy use, water, waste and circularity, climate-risk disclosure
S · SocialThe operation’s impact on peopleProduct safety, worker safety and diversity, supply-chain labor, community relations, data privacy
G · GovernanceHow power is kept in checkBoard independence and structure, shareholder rights, business ethics, anti-corruption, information disclosure
Five signals for spotting greenwashing
  • Vague wording: “green,” “eco-friendly,” “sustainable” — with no standard or definition ever offered;
  • Selective disclosure: a big story about one green production line, silence about total emissions and pollution;
  • No third-party verification: self-declared claims with no independent certification or audit cited;
  • All story, no data: vision and founding purpose in abundance — comparable numbers nowhere;
  • Contradictory conduct: the core business contradicts the marketing — expanding high-pollution capacity on one hand while paying record sums for green billboards on the other (illustrative).

Case: Johnson & Johnson’s 1982 Tylenol recall — a huge short-term loss exchanged for long-term trust (a classic)

In the fall of 1982, in the Chicago area of the United States, someone laced Tylenol capsules on retail shelves with cyanide; seven people died after taking them (public record). The deadly substance was not a fault of Johnson & Johnson’s manufacturing — the tampering happened after the product left the plant. The company faced a choice: wait for the investigation’s conclusions, or act immediately?

J&J chose the fastest possible action: an immediate nationwide withdrawal of roughly 31 million bottles of Tylenol (public records, approximate), with direct losses on the order of US$100 million at the time (illustrative); at the same time it communicated openly with the media and pioneered triple-seal tamper-evident packaging, lifting safety standards for the entire industry. In the short run Tylenol’s market share fell sharply; but according to public records, sales had roughly returned to pre-crisis levels within about two years (approximate), and the brand’s trust rose rather than fell — the “Tylenol decision” has been the opening case in business-ethics classrooms ever since.

What to notice in this case: the fault was not J&J’s, yet it still chose to put customer safety ahead of cost — stakeholder logic in action: short-term profit belongs to shareholders; long-term trust belongs to everyone. In a crisis, the asset called trust has two possible fates: be liquidated all at once, or appreciate because of one “money-losing” choice.

Key Terms

Shareholder primacy
The view that the firm’s first responsibility is to create profits for shareholders, associated with Friedman’s 1970 essay.
Stakeholder
All parties affected by the company’s operations: customers, employees, suppliers, communities, regulators, and shareholders.
Newspaper test
An ethical self-check: if this decision made tomorrow’s front page, could you face it calmly?
ESG
The sustainability-assessment framework built on three pillars: environmental, social, and governance.
Greenwashing
Dressing up an unchanged environmental record with vague, selective, or unverified green claims.
Long-termism
Steering decisions by long-horizon value rather than short-term profit, and treating trust as a core asset.
Key Takeaways

The law is the floor, ethics the steering wheel: pass the three gates in order — legal, compliant with policy, clear in conscience. Trust is the greatest asset: built by compound interest, destroyed in a single night.

International Business · Lesson 2

Tariffs and Exchange Rates

Lesson 1 showed that trade makes both sides better off — but goods must pass through two “revolving doors”: customs adds a markup (the tariff), and money must be converted (the exchange rate). How far each door is cranked, and to which notch, directly rewrites the profit and loss of both the import business and the export business — and it is also where politics and economics argue loudest.

1How a Tariff Raises the Domestic Price

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. The first step in analyzing it is accepting the “small-country assumption”: if a country’s imports are too small to move the world market, it is a price taker at the world price — whatever the world trades at is the price. Impose a tariff of ¥t on each unit, and the domestic price rises from Pw to Pw + t in a single step.

The moment the price rises, three things happen at once (in the language of supply and demand — see Lesson 1 of the Economics branch): domestic producers, seeing a higher selling price, are willing to produce more, and output climbs along the supply curve; consumers, seeing a higher price, buy less, and quantity demanded slides down the demand curve; the import volume squeezed between the two shrinks accordingly. A tariff works like a sluice gate: it shields the producers inside the gate, squeezes the consumers inside the gate, and shuts foreign competitors outside.

Domestic price with tariff Pt = world price Pw + tariff t

2Who Wins, Who Loses: The Welfare Ledger of a Tariff

A tariff creates no wealth out of thin air; what it does is transfer: it moves money from consumers to domestic producers and the government, “spilling” a portion along the way. Economists keep the books with three areas:

  • Consumer loss: as the price rises from Pw to Pt, consumer surplus shrinks by one whole trapezoid.
  • Producer gain and government revenue: the part of the trapezoid transferred to domestic firms (the green area left of the supply curve), plus the “tariff × import volume” rectangle the government collects.
  • Deadweight loss: the two red triangles inside the trapezoid that nobody receives — inefficient domestic production forcibly replaces cheaper imports, a slice of consumption is “priced out,” and total social welfare falls on net.
Deadweight loss = ½ × t × rise in domestic output (production-side triangle) + ½ × t × fall in domestic consumption (consumption-side triangle)

Why tax imports despite the known losses

Because the gains and the losses have different political visibility: producer gains are concentrated, well organized, and easy to see (factories and jobs saved), while consumer losses are scattered across everyone’s countless shopping trips and hardly anyone takes to the streets over them. Add the infant-industry, national-security, and anti-dumping arguments — each can hold under specific conditions — but on the accounting line that consumers ultimately pay the bill, most empirical studies agree (order-of-magnitude, illustrative).

3Exchange Rates: One Line of Digits Rewrites Two Businesses

Goods crossing a border need money to change hands, and the rate of that change is the exchange rate. Most countries use direct quotation: the domestic-currency price of one unit of foreign currency, as in “US$1 ≈ RMB 7.1” (approximate, illustrative). When the number rises (7.1 → 7.4), more domestic currency is needed to buy US$1 — the domestic currency depreciates; when it falls (7.1 → 6.9), the currency appreciates.

Link Lesson 1 with this one: comparative advantage decides whether to trade; tariffs and exchange rates decide at what price to trade. A 5% currency swing can swallow a 3% net margin on an export business in one bite — which is why income statements so often carry a line for “exchange gains and losses.”

4The Economic Logic of Trade Friction

When one country raises tariffs, the other usually retaliates; with both sides escalating, you have trade friction. The script is almost fixed. Round one: domestic producers gain and consumers lose. Round two: the partner retaliates and the home export industry takes the hit. Round three: supply chains on both sides start detouring — transshipment, factories in third countries, nearshoring — as firms pay a permanent restructuring cost for “policy risk.” In game-theory terms this is a classic prisoner’s dilemma: each side’s individually dominant choice (tax to protect) adds up to a worse result for both.

Case · US–China Trade Friction Since 2018 (illustrative, neutral view)

From 2018, acting on the findings of its domestic Section 301 investigation, the United States imposed tariffs on goods imported from China in successive batches, covering volumes in the hundreds of billions of US dollars (approximate, illustrative); China responded with retaliatory measures on goods imported from the US, tariff levels swung back and forth across multiple lists, and the process — punctuated by rounds of negotiation and phase agreements — dragged on for years with repeated reversals.

The general finding of public research (order-of-magnitude, illustrative): most of the tariff cost has been borne by US importers and consumers; some Chinese exporters saw margins squeezed as orders and capacity shifted toward Vietnam, Mexico, and elsewhere; and firms in both countries paid extra costs for compliance and supply-chain reconfiguration. Each government’s position stands on its own — one says it is correcting unfair trade and protecting its own industries and jobs, the other that it is defending free-trade rules and its own right to develop.

The lesson: political logic sees the “jobs saved,” while the economic ledger keeps recording “scattered consumer losses + deadweight loss + retaliation costs.” The other lesson globalized firms have learned: tariffs and geopolitics, like exchange rates, are first-class variables that must be written into supply-chain design.

Key Terms

Tariff
A tax on imported goods; it directly raises the domestic price and shrinks imports.
World Price
The price of a good on the international market; under the small-country assumption it sets the domestic price.
Consumer Surplus
The sum of the gaps between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay; a tariff shrinks it.
Producer Surplus
The sum of the gaps between the price firms receive and the lowest price they would accept; a tariff expands it.
Deadweight Loss
The net welfare loss that nobody receives under a tariff — the two triangles on the supply-and-demand diagram.
Direct Quotation
Quoting the exchange rate as the domestic-currency price of one unit of foreign currency; a rising number means the domestic currency is depreciating.
Appreciation & Depreciation
A currency’s purchasing power strengthening or weakening relative to another; the direction decides who gains, the exporting side or the importing side.
Trade Friction
A tit-for-tat cycle of tariffs and non-tariff barriers that usually lowers both sides’ welfare.
Key Takeaways

A tariff transfers wealth from scattered consumers to producers and the government, evaporating a slice of deadweight loss along the way; the exchange rate is the price face of the same coin — the headwind on exports is the tailwind on imports. Policies will change, but the arithmetic of these two ledgers does not.

Business Analytics · Lesson 1

Data Thinking: From Problem to Data

Open a spreadsheet with hundreds of thousands of rows: a novice’s first reaction is “Where do I even start?”, while a veteran’s is “What question am I answering?”. The business world has never lacked data — cash registers, customer-management systems, and advertising consoles record it automatically every day. What is missing is the thinking process that turns data into decisions. This lesson hands you that process — it holds its value better than any tool.

1The Five Steps of Analysis: From Business Question to Decision

Data Analysis in business never ends at a chart; it ends at a decision. A complete analytics pipeline runs in five steps: business question → decompose hypotheses → pull the data → analyze → drive the decision. Click each stage in the pipeline diagram below to see what it does, where it tends to go wrong, and what it produces:

Analytics Pipeline: Click Any Stage to Open Its Detail Card

The five stages link head to tail: each stage’s deliverable is the next stage’s input.

The costliest errors in the five steps happen at the two ends: if Step 1 defines the problem wrong, every later gain in precision is wasted; if Step 5’s conclusions never drive a decision, the analysis was merely “homework turned in”. The middle three steps are craft; the two ends are responsibility.

2What Makes a Good Question: Actionable and Owned

Concept Card · What Makes a Good Analytics Question

Two yardsticks. Actionability — different values of the answer should point to different actions; if you would do nothing whether the answer turns out A or B, that is not a question, it is curiosity. Ownership — a specific person is accountable for the answer and has the authority to push the follow-up actions. Counter-example: “Why don’t users stay?” (too broad, nobody owns it). Good example: “New-user 7-day retention fell 3 percentage points month over month — at which step after signup is the loss concentrated?” (metric, scope, baseline, and time frame all present, and a product owner claims it).

“Why” questions need to be rewritten as “which explanation does the data support” — because “why” cannot be answered directly; it can only be broken into several testable hypotheses for the data to eliminate one by one.

3Metrics and Dimensions: The Two Axes of Data

A Metric is “a computed number” that answers how much: sales, conversion rate, retention rate. A Dimension is “an angle for slicing” that answers viewed from where: region, channel, category, time. Slice the same metric along different dimensions, and “total sales fell” becomes “dairy sales fell in the online channel in the East China region” — exactly the seed of Lesson 4’s drill-down.

Data Literacy = asking good questions > mastering tools. Excel, SQL, and Python get replaced every few years; the ability to translate a business concern into a data question stays scarce forever. When an interviewer asks “what tools do you know”, what they really want to know is whether you can pull the right question out of the fog.

4Worked Walk-Through: “Sales Are Down — How Do We Analyze It?”

Let’s run this most common of questions through the five-step process (figures are classroom illustrations). Scroll down to expand each step:

  1. Define the question. Storewide monthly sales fell about 12% month over month (illustrative), scope limited to the online channel, observation window the last 8 weeks. Output: a one-sentence problem definition plus a named owner.
  2. Decompose into hypotheses. Sales = traffic × conversion rate × average order value; three candidate explanations: a competitor promotion diverted traffic, stockouts of best-sellers depressed conversion, reduced promotion of high-ticket categories dragged average order value down.
  3. Pull the data. Bring up 8 weeks of the three metrics by category and by day, plus the promotion calendar; agree on the “sales including tax” definition first, then check for missing days.
  4. Analyze. Traffic is roughly flat; average order value fell about 8%; the steepest volume drops sit in high-ticket categories and coincide with the stockout records — the stockout hypothesis holds (illustrative).
  5. Drive the decision. Restocking priority for high-ticket categories is raised, a weekly stockout alert goes live, and the metrics get reviewed after two weeks. Only now does the analysis close its loop.
Case · 7-Eleven: Running Hypothesis–Act–Verify in Every Convenience Store

A convenience store must answer a thousand small questions a day: how many extra rice balls to order today? What goes on the second shelf? 7-Eleven’s Item-by-Item Management treats every SKU (Stock Keeping Unit, the smallest unit of inventory) as a hypothesis: ordering is the hypothesis, selling out is the verification, sell-outs and waste are error signals. Championed by Toshifumi Suzuki, this loop — paired with information systems and temperature-controlled logistics — let stores order by hypothesis–act–verify rather than pure gut feel (a synthesis of public sources, illustrative). Site selection works the same way: not “a feeling”, but first quantifying district foot traffic, customer mix, and competitor distribution, then answering “who is this store for”.

The lesson: data thinking is not a technology, it is a work habit — break a big worry into small, specific, data-refutable hypotheses and speed the cycle up. Once the question is well defined, Lesson 2’s statistical tools have a target to hit.

Key Terms

Metric
A quantity computed from data that answers “how much” — e.g. sales, conversion rate, retention rate.
Dimension
An angle for slicing and viewing a metric — e.g. region, channel, category, time.
Actionability
One criterion of a good question: different values of the answer point to different actions.
Ownership
One criterion of a good question: a clear owner is accountable for the answer and empowered to push follow-up actions.
Hypothesis
A candidate explanation for the question, written so data can test it — and refute it.
Data Literacy
The ability to translate business concerns into data questions and to read conclusions critically.
Item Management
The ordering and shelf-management loop of hypothesis–act–verify run at the level of individual SKUs.
Key Takeaways

Ask the right question before touching the data: a good question is actionable and owned; walk the five steps — question → hypotheses → data → analysis → decision, and any missing link leaves a half-finished product; people who can ask questions will always be worth more than people who can only operate tools.

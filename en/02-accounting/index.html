Accounting · The Language of Business

Accounting

Someone who cannot read financial statements will forever hear about a company’s health secondhand. Accounting is the shared language of the business world — four lessons take you from its first sentence, Assets = Liabilities + Owners’ Equity, all the way to the DuPont analysis (ROE split into three factors).

This branch deliberately avoids rote memorization of debit-credit direction tables and focuses on four things: intuition for the equation, how the three statements interlock, what to look at first when you are handed a set of statements, and how ratios give a company its checkup. You may not be able to keep books when you finish — but you will certainly be able to read them, which is exactly the part managers, investors, and founders need.

1Sub-branch Map

Accounting divides, broadly, into four sub-fields: financial accounting, managerial accounting, auditing, and tax. The four lessons in this branch cover the practical main line — financial accounting’s recording, statement preparation, and analysis; the remaining directions are listed as advanced signposts, for digging deeper once you finish.

2Learning Path: Why This Order

From the alphabet to the book review

Lesson 1 The Accounting Equation → Lesson 2 The Three Statements → Lesson 3 Reading Statements → Lesson 4 Financial Ratios

The equation is the alphabet, the accounts are the words, the statements are the sentences, reading statements is comprehension, and ratios are the book review — skip any layer, and the ones above it collapse into rote memorization.

Each lesson answers exactly one question. Lesson 1: how does one transaction become two entries? Lesson 2: what slice of time does each statement see, and how do they check against one another? Lesson 3: when you are handed an income statement, where do your eyes land first? Lesson 4: how do you compress three statements into a few comparable ratios and give the company a checkup?

4Connections to Other Branches

Prerequisite · 01 Economics

Opportunity cost, fixed versus variable costs, marginal analysis — these economics tools are the bedrock for understanding cost structure and accounting decisions. You can start without them, but studying them first makes everything clearer.

Next up · 03 Finance

Statements are the raw material of valuation: every valuation model is ultimately “discounting the cash flows of the future,” and those cash flows start from the three statements. Enter finance after this branch, and you will know exactly which line of which statement each valuation number grew out of.

Side echoes: Lesson 4’s inventory turnover and receivables turnover connect directly to 07 Operations & Supply Chain; the question “can the statements be trusted?” opens up in 12 Business Law & Ethics (corporate governance and auditing).

Market Structures: From Perfect Competition to Monopoly

Why can’t a vegetable vendor charge a ‘brand premium’ for greens, while the iPhone dares to sell for over ¥10,000? Why have the two cola giants fought an advertising war for a century yet rarely fought a real price war? The answer lies not in the products but in market structure — the competitive environment you operate in has already determined how much money you can make.

1Market structure: the ranking ladder of competition

The first question of business strategy is not ‘how do I fight’ but ‘what does the battlefield look like’. Within an industry, the ‘elasticity’ from Lesson 2 determines how easily your customers can flee, while market structure determines how many rivals you have, whether the product can be differentiated, whether new players can enter, and whether you hold pricing power. Economics arranges markets along a spectrum by four dimensions: perfect competition → monopolistic competition → oligopoly → monopoly.

Price Taker

A firm in a perfectly competitive market: its share is too small, so it must take the going price — raise your greens 50% at a wet market and customers simply turn to the next stall. In the long run, competition squeezes economic profit toward zero.

Price Maker

A monopoly (and some oligopoly) firm: facing the entire downward-sloping demand curve, it can ‘set the price first, then choose the quantity’. It has no rivals, yet remains constrained by two things: the law of demand (price too high and fewer buy) and government regulation / antitrust.

What underpins pricing power is entry barriers: economies of scale, patents and licenses, brand and switching costs, and network effects — products that get more useful the more people use them (social networks, payments) naturally slide toward winner-take-all. The higher the barriers, the longer profits persist; high profits without barriers are simply an invitation to future entrants.

3Case: three markets, three fates

The wet market, the cola duopoly, and the power grid

The wet market (near-perfect competition): dozens of stalls sell nearly identical vegetables, information is transparent, and entry and exit are free. Each stall owner is a price taker with only a few cents of bargaining room; the edge comes from a sharp eye at the early-morning wholesale market and a good stall location, not pricing power.

The cola duopoly: Coca-Cola and Pepsi together hold roughly 70% of the global carbonated-soft-drink market (order of magnitude, illustrative); they are mutually dependent and watch each other closely — if one cuts price, the other follows, and a price war wounds both, so the battlefield shifts to advertising, sponsorships, and distribution channels. This is the classic strategic interaction of oligopoly.

State Grid and WeChat (monopoly): a transmission grid is asset-heavy and uneconomic to duplicate — the textbook natural monopoly — and is therefore subject to strict government price regulation. WeChat, powered by network effects, dominates acquaintance-based social networking: the product is free, yet across payments, advertising, and the mini-program ecosystem it wields enormous pricing and rule-setting power — and accordingly faces continuing antitrust scrutiny.

The lesson: structure decides fate — your pricing power equals ‘the degree to which your customers cannot find substitutes’. Much of what corporate strategy does (brand, patents, ecosystems) is, in essence, an effort to move the firm from the left end of the spectrum to the right.

Key Terms

Perfect Competition
Countless firms selling homogeneous products with free entry and exit; firms are price takers.
Monopolistic Competition
Many firms with differentiated products; differentiation earns a sliver of pricing power.
Oligopoly
A few large firms that are mutually dependent, each decision restraining the others.
Monopoly
A single firm owns the whole market; the product has no close substitutes.
Entry Barriers
Factors that keep new firms out: economies of scale, patents, licenses, brand, network effects.
Price Taker
A firm with no pricing power that must transact at the market price.
Price Maker
A firm able to set its own price and then decide its quantity.
Key Takeaways

The ceiling on profit is set by market structure: the more rivals and the lower the barriers, the less pricing power you have. The essence of competitive strategy is to move yourself from the price-taker box into the price-maker box.

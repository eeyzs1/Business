Bottlenecks and the Theory of Constraints

In the last lesson you already met the bottleneck in a tea shop: the slowest station sets the whole line’s output. This lesson upgrades that intuition into a complete management philosophy — the Theory of Constraints — and answers an uncomfortable question: why is money spent on the fast steps almost always money wasted?

1The Factory That Got Busier and Poorer

In 1984 Eliyahu Goldratt, a physicist turned consultant, wrote the business novel The Goal: plant manager Alex Rogo’s factory is full of busy people and fully loaded machines, yet it teeters on the edge of closure. The mentor Jonah asks a single question: “Where is your bottleneck?” From there unfolds the core proposition of the Theory of Constraints (TOC):

The Core Proposition of the Theory of Constraints

The output of any system is determined by a tiny number of constraints — its bottlenecks — just as a chain’s strength is determined by its weakest link. Therefore: improving a non-bottleneck does not increase system output; whatever extra it produces cannot pass the bottleneck, and only becomes piled-up inventory and money spent early. Managing a system means, first of all, managing its constraints.

Why is “everyone busy” actually bad news? Because a busy non-bottleneck is, in essence, building an ever-longer queue in front of the bottleneck. TOC even keeps a different set of books — throughput accounting: watch only three numbers. Throughput (T): the money generated by what you sell. Inventory (I): the money locked inside the system. Operating expense (OE): the money spent turning inventory into throughput. The direction of profit is to raise T while lowering I and OE — and speeding up a non-bottleneck usually raises I while T doesn’t move.

2Why Improving Non-Bottlenecks Is Useless, Even Harmful

Picture a five-station line as five pipe segments of different diameters: the flow is set by the narrowest one. Swap a wide segment for an even wider one and the flow doesn’t change by a drop; but upstream water now surges toward the narrow segment faster and pools in front of it. In a factory, that pool is work-in-process (WIP) inventory: it ties up cash, it eats floor space, and it hides defects (as Lesson 3 will show, inventory is the problem’s cover-up).

The more insidious damage is in decision-making: the “efficiency” figures of non-bottlenecks look better, management concludes the system is improving, and keeps investing in the wrong place. TOC’s advice: let non-bottlenecks produce only as needed and allow them to sit idle — that is not waste, it is discipline. That is the essence of the drum-buffer-rope (DBR) scheduling idea: set the whole line’s rhythm by the bottleneck’s drumbeat (drum), hold a small buffer of inventory in front of the bottleneck so it never starves (buffer), and use a “rope” to throttle the rate of material release so upstream cannot run wild (rope).

3The Five Focusing Steps: A Methodology for Living with Constraints

  1. Identify — find the system’s true bottleneck: the longest queue and the biggest pile of WIP usually mark the spot.
  2. Exploit — without spending a cent, first squeeze the existing capacity of the bottleneck dry: never let it sit idle waiting for parts, never schedule changeovers during bottleneck time, move inspection and maintenance off the bottleneck’s hours, never let it work on defective units.
  3. Subordinate — make everything else march to the bottleneck’s rhythm: release material to the bottleneck’s “drumbeat,” and let the fast steps stand idle if necessary.
  4. Elevate — only when the first three steps are exhausted do you spend money on more capacity: add equipment, add shifts, outsource the overflow.
  5. Repeat — once broken, the bottleneck moves elsewhere; guard against inertia and return to step one, rather than keep optimizing a constraint that no longer exists.
The order is the pointFirst exploit and subordinate, elevate last — buying capacity is always the final step. Most companies do exactly the opposite: they see a queue and buy equipment, and the new capacity piles up a fresh pool of inventory.

5Case: The Slowest Scout Sets the Pace of the Whole Troop

The Scout Hike in The Goal

The novel’s most famous scene does not take place in the factory but on a mountain trail. Plant manager Alex Rogo takes a troop of scouts hiking, and the column keeps stretching: the fast kids amble at the front and wait, the slow kids scramble at the back to catch up, and the line stretches like a rubber band — the hikers up front are “idle” almost the whole way. The moment the whole troop reaches camp is decided entirely by the slowest, plump boy in the group: Herbie.

Alex’s solution is the five focusing steps in miniature. First identify — Herbie is the bottleneck. Then exploit — redistribute the heavy items in Herbie’s backpack to the others, and he immediately speeds up. Then subordinate — march the whole troop at Herbie’s pace and place him in the middle, and the column stops stretching apart. Only if that is still not fast enough comes elevate — say, buying Herbie a good pair of boots (the factory equivalent of buying equipment).

The operations lesson: “Herbie’s backpack” is the template for bottleneck improvement in any organization: do the free reallocation and resequencing first (exploit, subordinate), and only then talk investment (elevate). Misidentify the Herbie, or lighten the load of a kid who was already fast, and the troop arrives at camp not one minute sooner. (This episode is a fictional illustration from the novel)

Key Terms

Theory of Constraints (TOC)
The methodology that holds that system output is determined by a very few constraints, and that managing means continually identifying and exploiting them.
Bottleneck / Constraint
The step with the smallest capacity, which sets output for the whole system; where the queues and the WIP pile up.
Non-Bottleneck Resource
A step with more capacity than the system needs; running it at full load only produces inventory.
Throughput (T)
The rate of output realized through sales — the rate at which the system actually makes money.
Inventory (I)
Materials in the system, finished and in process: the money locked inside the chain.
Operating Expense (OE)
Everything the system spends to turn inventory into throughput.
Five Focusing Steps
The constraint-improvement loop: identify → exploit → subordinate → elevate → repeat.
Drum-Buffer-Rope (DBR)
The scheduling idea: the bottleneck’s beat is the drum, a buffer sits in front of the bottleneck, and a rope limits material release.
Key Takeaways
System output is set by the bottleneck, just as a chain is set by its weakest link: improving a non-bottleneck adds no output, only inventory. First exploit and subordinate; spend money to elevate only at the end — then repeat, because the new bottleneck is already waiting somewhere else.

STP: Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning

“Our product is for everyone” — that sentence almost always means “for no one”. Every company’s resources are finite: the ad budget is finite, channel shelf space is finite, R&D bandwidth is finite. STP is the spine of marketing strategy, and it forces you to answer three questions first: how to slice the market into segments (S), which segments to compete in (T), and what to say to customers inside the ones you choose (P) — in short, “to whom, saying what”.

1Segmentation (S): The Market Is Not One Whole Cake

Market segmentation means cutting the apparently “everyone” total market into groups that are similar inside and different from one another, along differences in customer characteristics. Four families of segmentation variables are in common use:

Variable familyWhat you look atBeverage-market example
DemographicAge, gender, income, occupation, family structureUrban women aged 18–30
GeographicCity tier, climate, regional tastesOffice districts in tier-1 and tier-2 cities
PsychographicValues, lifestyle, personalitySugar-watching fitness buffs; ingredient-label readers
BehavioralUsage occasion, frequency, loyalty, benefits soughtThe daily afternoon-tea occasion; chasing “zero burden”

In practice the families are usually combined — “tier-1 city + aged 25–35 + healthy lifestyle + several drinks a week”, for instance. A good segment must be identifiable, reachable, and worth a dedicated set of moves — otherwise slicing it is the same as not slicing it at all.

2Targeting (T): Pick the Battles Worth Fighting

Targeting means trading off between segments; three yardsticks do most of the work:

  1. Is the size right? (Segment Size): too small and the market cannot carry the business model; too big and you may not have the teeth for it.
  2. Can you reach them? (Accessibility): can existing channels and media actually get to these people — and at what cost?
  3. Does it fit? (Fit / Sustainability): does the segment match the company’s capabilities, resources, and long-term direction, and can you build a durable advantage there?

From there the firm chooses a coverage strategy: focus on a single segment (concentrated marketing), cover several chosen segments (differentiated marketing), or run one plan across the whole market (undifferentiated marketing). New brands with limited resources usually enter through a single segment.

3Positioning (P): Claim a Slot in the Customer’s Mind

Concept card · Positioning

Positioning is not “what you think you are”; it is your location relative to competitors inside the target customer’s mind. In Ries and Trout’s famous formulation, positioning is seizing an open slot on the ladder in the customer’s mind. The standard positioning-statement template: “For (the target segment), (brand) is the (point-of-difference) choice in (the category), because (reason to believe).”

The perceptual map is positioning’s workbench: put the two dimensions customers genuinely care about on the axes (say price × healthiness) and draw the category’s brands as bubbles — bubble size stands for scale (illustrative). Dense, crowded zones are red oceans; unoccupied blank space may be an opportunity — but first ask: is the blank there because nobody thought of it, or because there is no demand here?

Case · Genki Forest: “0-sugar sparkling water” won on a perceptual map

S · Segmentation: the sparkling-water market had long been split between pricey imported mineral sparkling water and sugary carbonated soft drinks. Sliced by “health appeal × taste appeal”, there was a crowd of young people who “want sweet, fizzy refreshment while watching their sugar” (urban office workers aged 25–35, gym-goers, ingredient-label readers) — and the cola giants’ sugar-free lines either tasted limited or still read as “cola” in the mind.

T · Targeting: the segment was swelling as health awareness spread; convenience stores, e-commerce, and content platforms kept reach costs manageable; and the team’s strengths in internet playbooks and new channels made for a strong fit — all three yardsticks passed.

P · Positioning: using erythritol, it built a sparkling water with “0 sugar, 0 calories, 0 fat”, dressed it in minimalist Japanese-style packaging, claimed the open “health × fashion” quadrant, and said it in one line: “guilt-free fizz”. After launch, sales climbed from roughly ¥200 million to around ¥2.7 billion by 2020 (public reporting, illustrative), and several giants followed with sugar-free lines of their own — the position, validated by the market.

The lesson: STP is not a form-filling ritual; it is the entry algorithm by which a new brand fights above its weight: segmentation finds the people, targeting runs the numbers, positioning claims the open slot — three interlocking steps.

Key Terms

Segmentation
Slicing the total market into internally similar customer groups using demographic, geographic, psychographic, and behavioral variables.
Targeting
Evaluating segments with three yardsticks — size, accessibility, and fit — and choosing which to serve.
Positioning
Occupying a clear, favorable place in the target customer’s mind, relative to competitors.
Perceptual Map
A plot of brands’ relative positions on two dimensions customers care about; often used to spot open space.
Segmentation Variables
The customer characteristics used to slice the market: demographic, geographic, psychographic, and behavioral.
Differentiation
The set of value-creating moves designed around the position, giving customers a perceivable difference from rivals.
White Space
An unoccupied area on the perceptual map — possibly an opportunity, possibly a false blank with no demand.
Key Takeaways

Marketing strategy in one line: segment first (S), select next (T), position last (P). Segment with the four families of variables, select with the three yardsticks, position by finding open space on the perceptual map inside the customer’s mind — until you can answer “to whom, saying what”, every marketing action just spins its wheels.

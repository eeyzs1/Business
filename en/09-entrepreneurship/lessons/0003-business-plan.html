The Business Plan: Think Before You Launch

Once validation has produced conclusions, you need to turn them into a document others can follow — to persuade investors, to align co-founders, and to force yourself to lay every vague spot on the table. This lesson takes apart the business plan (BP for short): who reads it, its standard structure, and its most concentrated form — the 30-second elevator pitch.

1Who Reads a BP: Three Audiences, Three Faces

Startup circles love to say “skip the business plan,” because plans cannot keep up with change, and a fifty-page document expires in three weeks. That is only half right: a BP’s value was never predictive accuracy, but the way writing it forces you to think clearly. The same draft wears three different faces for three audiences:

ReaderWhat they most want to confirmWhat you should emphasize
InvestorsIs this big enough? Why you? Do risk and reward match?How big the problem is, the growth path, the team’s fit, and what the raise will accomplish
Co-founders / key candidatesIs the direction credible? What is my place and my return?Vision, division of roles, equity arrangements, decision rules
YourselfWhich parts have I actually not thought through?The list of assumptions and the points at risk of being falsified — this version is honest only to you
Exercise In the version you write for yourself, tag every claim as either “validated” or “I believe.” The lines dominated by “I believe” are exactly what your next MVP should test.

2The Standard Structure: Eight Pieces

Templates differ slightly across investment firms, but the skeleton is remarkably consistent. As you read, switch to the other side of the table — when an investor turns to each page, a question is already running through their mind:

#ModuleWhat the investor is thinking on this page
1ProblemWhose problem is this, and how painful? Why did you spot it before anyone else?
2SolutionWhere is the tenfold improvement over current practice? Why are you the one who can build it?
3MarketHow big is the first pot of gold, counted bottom-up — not the industry’s total size
4Business ModelWho pays, how you charge, the gross-margin structure, and the repeat-purchase logic
5CompetitionWho are the substitutes? What if a giant does this as a side project?
6TeamWhat makes this team a match made in heaven for this problem?
7FinancialsIs the key-assumption-driven projection internally consistent (not a precise prophecy)?
8The AskHow much money, what stake given up, and which milestones it buys

Note the intent behind the order: the first two pages win the reader over on “real problem, clever solution,” and only then do team and financials have a context to be judged in. Opening with praise for the team is the most common layout mistake beginners make.

3The Elevator Pitch: Eight Pieces Compressed into One Sentence

For [target customers] we solve [pain point] with [solution],
unlike [alternatives] and their [key difference]

The elevator pitch gets its name from a scene: you meet an investor in an elevator, and you have thirty seconds. The sentence works because it forces you to answer the four most critical blocks of the BP at once: market, problem, solution, competition. The most neglected of these is alternatives — without saying what you are compared with, the listener cannot place your business.

Three Real-World Style Examples, Compared

These are style reconstructions based on public reporting, not verbatim company quotes.

AIRBNB-STYLE · CIRCA 2008

For budget-conscious travelers we solve the pain of lodging that is pricey and identical everywhere, through staying in local hosts’ spare rooms — unlike cookie-cutter chain budget hotels and their monotonous standard rooms, giving you a cheaper and more authentic local experience.

XIAOMI-STYLE · CIRCA 2010

For young buyers chasing value for money we solve the pain of flagship phones that cost too much, through online direct sales with high specs at low prices — unlike offline channels that mark up at every layer and their channel premium, selling the same specs at half the price.

DIDI-STYLE · CIRCA 2012

For city commuters in a hurry we solve the pain of no taxi to be found on rainy days, through one tap on your phone to hail a nearby vacant car — unlike standing at the curb waving and hoping and its uncertainty, with a car confirmed to pick you up within minutes.

Case Study · The One-Page Plan: Turning the BP from Thesis to Whiteboard

What an early team really needs is usually not a 40-page document but a list of assumptions that can be pinned to the wall and scribbled on at any time. The exemplar of the one-page plan is the Lean Canvas: Ash Maurya lightly reworked Osterwalder’s business model canvas (see Lesson 4 of the 08 Strategy branch) into nine boxes — problem, customer segments, unique value proposition, solution, channels, revenue, costs, key metrics, and unfair advantage — letting a founder lay out every assumption of a business on a whiteboard in twenty minutes and align with co-founders box by box.

Even giants lean on the value of “writing it down”: for years, Amazon has replaced meeting slides with roughly six-page narrative memos — because complete sentences expose the holes in your thinking, and animated transitions do not. The one-page plan is the startup edition of the same logic: the shorter the document, the less room vagueness has to hide.

The lesson: the value of a plan lies in the writing; the document is a by-product. Align assumptions on one page first, then expand into the eight-part external version — reverse the order and you are just “writing for writing’s sake.”

Key Terms

Business Plan
A document that systematically presents the problem, solution, market, model, competition, team, financials, and funding ask.
Elevator Pitch
A concentrated statement that explains a business in about 30 seconds, unfolding as customer–pain–solution–comparison–difference.
Executive Summary
The one- or two-page digest at the front of a BP; most investors read only this part.
Lean Canvas
A one-page, nine-box whiteboard tool that condenses a business model’s assumptions for fast alignment and iteration.
Alternatives
The other approaches customers use when your product is absent; the frame of reference that positions you in a pitch.
The Ask
The amount this round intends to raise, the stake offered, and the milestones the money will buy.
Key Takeaways

A BP is at heart a record of thinking: the eight-part structure answers “does the business hold together,” and the elevator pitch answers “can you explain it in thirty seconds.” Align assumptions on one page first, then expand into the document.

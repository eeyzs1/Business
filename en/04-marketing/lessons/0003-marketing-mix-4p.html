The 4 Ps: Orchestrating the Marketing Mix

Lesson 2’s STP answered “to whom, saying what”; this lesson answers “what exactly do we do”. The marketing mix’s 4 Ps — product, price, place, promotion — codified by E. Jerome McCarthy, are the four conductor’s batons that translate positioning strategy into moves customers can feel. They are not four independent knobs but a set of interlocking gears: turn one, and the other three must turn with it.

1Product (P): Peeling the Three-Layer Onion

Customers never buy just “the thing”. A product (Product) has at least three layers:

In the most fiercely contested markets, actual products converge — and the augmented layer becomes the main battleground of differentiation, which is why the profit pool hidden in services is so often overlooked.

2Price (P): The Most Sensitive Lever

Pricing (Price) has three basic orientations: cost-based (markup over cost), competition-based (benchmarked to rivals), and value-based (anchored to customer-perceived value). None is absolutely right or wrong, but only the value orientation dares to answer “what is the customer’s inner scale actually worth?” — echoing Lesson 1’s value subtraction.

Price is so sensitive because it runs straight into price elasticity of demand — an old friend from the 01 Economics branch. Elasticity e describes “raise price 1%, and volume falls how many percent”. In the simplified world of constant elasticity, the profit-maximizing price has an elegant closed form:

Optimal price P* = unit cost C × ee − 1(e > 1)

The intuition: the more elastic demand (more substitutes, fiercer competition), the thinner the optimal markup 1/(e−1) — which is why commodities dare not raise price while one-of-a-kind products do. Time to turn the knobs yourself.

3Place and Promotion: Reach and Persuade

Place answers “where does the customer buy it”: direct selling (DTC — the brand’s own stores, site, and app; controlled experience, richer margin), distribution (riding dealers and retail networks; fast shelf coverage, profit shared away), and omni-channel (online and offline fused, one inventory serving every touchpoint). The promotion mix answers “how do we get believed”: advertising (paying for reach), public relations (building credibility through third-party voices), sales promotion (coupons, trials, and other short-term incentives), and personal selling (face-to-face, tailored persuasion). Blending the four tools around the target audience’s media habits is integrated marketing communications.

Case · Early Xiaomi: a tightly interlocking 4-P combination

When the first Xiaomi phone launched in 2011, the moves interlocked. Product: flagship-grade chip specs as the actual product’s selling point, weekly MIUI updates and forum co-creation as the augmented product, with “value for money” engineered into the core benefit. Price: at ¥1,999, roughly half the going rate of comparable international flagships at the time (illustrative), reverse-engineered from “cost + expected scale” and leaving almost no margin for channel middlemen.

Place: layers of distributors were cut; direct online sales through its own site plus limited-time open reservations turned the saved channel markup straight into price advantage — first-round reservations ran to about 300,000 units (public reporting, illustrative). Promotion: no television ad blitz; the message traveled through forum word of mouth, social media, and the “born for enthusiasts” community narrative. The four Ps fed one another: the direct channel supported the low price, the low price amplified the buzz, and the buzz fed the community back.

The lesson: the power of the 4 Ps lives in the word “combination”. No single P explains Xiaomi: the low price collapses without the direct channel, and the direct channel has no traffic without community-driven word of mouth. Judge any single move inside the combination, never alone.

Key Terms

Marketing Mix
The set of controllable marketing variables a company orchestrates; the classic framework is the 4 Ps.
Core Product
The fundamental benefit the customer is really buying; the innermost layer of the product.
Augmented Product
Additional services and experiences beyond basic expectations; the usual battleground of differentiation.
Value-Based Pricing
Setting price against customer-perceived value rather than against cost or competitors.
Price Elasticity of Demand
How sensitively volume responds to a price change; it sets how thick the optimal markup can be.
Omni-channel
A channel setup in which inventory, membership, and experience are unified across online and offline.
The 4 Cs
Robert Lauterborn’s customer-view counterpart to the 4 Ps: customer needs, cost, convenience, communication.
Key Takeaways

The 4 Ps are the combination that translates positioning into moves: the product has three layers, price is anchored to value while respecting elasticity, place decides reach, and promotion completes persuasion. Before changing any one P, ask whether the other three can keep up.

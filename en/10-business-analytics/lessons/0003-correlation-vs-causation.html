Correlation Is Not Causation

In the months when ice cream sells best, drowning accidents peak — should we ban ice cream? Correlation is the easiest relationship to observe in the data world, and the easiest to misuse: the marketing budget doubled and sales rose — who deserves the credit? This lesson bolts a three-stage filter onto “moving together” and upgrades it into the serious judgment of “who causes whom” — the most expensive checkpoint in data-driven decision-making.

1The Correlation Coefficient: The Attraction Between Numbers

Concept Card · Correlation Coefficient

Usually the Pearson correlation coefficient r, which measures how strongly two variables move together in a straight line, ranging from −1 to 1: the closer to 1, the tighter the same-direction movement; the closer to −1, the tighter the opposite-direction movement; near 0, the weaker the linear relationship. It only describes “moving together” and never answers “who drives whom”. When two variables correlate, three explanations are common: A causes B; B causes A; or a third variable C causes both A and B — C is the Confounder. A false correlation manufactured by C is a Spurious Correlation.

3From Correlation to Causation: Three Steps Up

To upgrade “correlation” to “causation”, you must clear at least three gates:

  1. Rule out confounding. After grouping, or statistically controlling for, the suspicious C, does the correlation still stand? Control for age, and the correlation between shoe size and reading scores vanishes.
  2. Check the time order. Cause must precede effect: ads run first and sales rise after — only then does causation even qualify; a reversed order can only be coincidence or reverse causation.
  3. Tell a plausible mechanism. There must be a transmission chain that makes sense — why exactly would an orange button be more credible? A strong correlation with no explainable mechanism is a lead, not a verdict.

Even clearing all three gates can still leave you wrong, which is why the gold standard in business is the Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT): randomly split people into two groups and change exactly one variable. Randomization flattens both the known and the unknown confounders at once. The internet turned the RCT into a daily operation — the A/B test: one group of users sees version A, another sees version B, and you compare the final metric.

Experiment discipline
Change only one variable at a time (the single-variable principle); fix the sample size before the test starts, and do not “peek every day and stop the moment it looks significant” — repeated peeking sharply inflates false positives; evaluate on the final business metric, not on click rate alone.

4Survivorship Bias: The Planes That Did Not Come Back

In World War II, the statistician Abraham Wald studied the distribution of bullet holes on bombers that returned — and recommended reinforcing the areas that had no holes, because the planes hit in those places never made it back. That is Survivorship Bias: counting only the sample that “survived” guarantees an overly rosy conclusion. Business versions are everywhere: survey only long-time users and you will never learn why the churned left; study only the “common traits” of successful companies and you may just be listening to survivors speak on luck’s behalf.

Case · Bing’s Blue Links: A Textbook A/B Test

The most tempting experiment in e-commerce is “change the button color, lift conversion”. Microsoft Bing once publicly shared one: A/B tests on many shades of blue for its search-result links, and the winning “bluer blue” reportedly added about US$80 million a year in incremental advertising revenue (a public-talk figure, illustrative). The point is not color mysticism but process discipline: a single variable (only the shade changed), random traffic splits (the system assigns users at random), enough sample (run the full pre-planned period), and a final metric (overall revenue, not momentary clicks). The cautionary tale looks like this: “We shipped the orange button on Friday, weekend sales rose 10%, so orange works” — with no control group, promotions, weather, and payday can all sneak into the result; that is exactly the missing “decompose hypotheses” step from Lesson 1.

The lesson: put every redesign proposal through four questions first: Can we split traffic randomly? Did we change only one variable? Is the sample big enough? What is the final metric? If an experiment is impossible, fall back to observational methods — grouped comparison, ruling out confounders, hunting for a mechanism — and say out loud how much confidence the conclusion carries.

Key Terms

Correlation Coefficient
The statistic r measuring how strongly two variables move together linearly, from −1 to 1; describes co-movement only, never direction.
Spurious Correlation
An apparent correlation created by a third factor or by coincidence; remove the confounder and it vanishes.
Confounder
A third factor that influences both variables — suspect number one on the road from correlation to causation.
Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT)
An experimental design with random group assignment and a single-variable contrast; the gold standard of causal inference.
A/B Testing
The internet’s RCT: randomly split users between two versions and compare the final metric.
Statistical Significance
A difference larger than noise can explain; business judgments often use the simplified 2σ rule.
Survivorship Bias
Systematic over-optimism from counting only the surviving samples, e.g. surveying only retained users.
Key Takeaways

Correlation is a lead, not a verdict: causation must clear three gates — confounding, time order, mechanism; run an experiment whenever you can, because the A/B test is the business world’s gold standard; before you judge, ask about the planes that did not come back.

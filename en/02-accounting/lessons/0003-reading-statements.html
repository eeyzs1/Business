Reading Financial Statements: Structure and Method

People who know how to “read” statements do not recite the numbers from top to bottom — they drill down by structure: first how big the cake is, then how much each layer carves away. This lesson hands you a simplified income statement and lights up every line — what it says and how to read it — then adds two reference rulers: year-over-year and sequential growth.

1Read the Structure First: Who Pays, and Where the Money Goes

The numbers on an income statement change with scale, but the structure exposes the shape of the business itself. The revenue mix answers “whose pockets does the money come from”: split it by product line, by region, by customer type, and you will see what the company truly leans on. The cost structure answers “whose pockets does the money flow to”: what share goes to materials, labor, rent, marketing — which items move with sales volume (variable costs) and which never budge (fixed costs).

Why structure matters more than numbers

A company’s revenue climbs from ¥10 billion to ¥12 billion — is that good news or bad? If the entire increase came from one money-losing promotion, the structure turns ugly before the profit does. Numbers are the outcome; structure is the cause — which is why analysts always flip to the segment data first.

2Three Layers of Profit: Read It Like Peeling an Onion

From top to bottom, an income statement is not a running log but a three-stage “peeling”:

Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold
Operating Profit = Gross Profit − Period Expenses
Net Income = Operating Profit − Finance Costs, Tax, etc.

Each layer manages one stretch: gross margin measures whether the product itself is worth money (pricing power); operating margin measures how well the business is run (expense control); net margin is the share that finally lands in the pocket after every layer is peeled away. Whichever layer “suddenly collapses” is where the problem lives — a gross-profit collapse means the product has fallen from favor, an operating-profit collapse means expenses are out of control, and a net-income collapse usually means a shock to interest or tax.

When printing or reviewing, retell the statement in these five steps.

4Year-over-Year and Sequential: Finding a Frame of Reference

A single period’s number has no context: “up 12%” must answer “compared with what?” Year-over-year (YoY) compares with the same period this year last year and washes out seasonality — cold drinks are always quiet in winter. Sequential (Seq., versus the prior period) captures the freshest momentum — turning points show up here first. Use the two rulers together: sequential gains for several quarters while YoY still falls can be an early signal of recovery.

5Case Study: Two Businesses, 90% and 20%

Premium baijiu vs. budget grocery: two worlds of gross margin (orders of magnitude, illustrative)

Put two companies you have heard of into one table: a premium baijiu producer (Kweichow Moutai, say) runs a gross margin around 90% year after year, while a budget grocery chain (retailers like Walmart or Yonghui) typically sits near 20% (both approximate, illustrative orders of magnitude). Why is the gap so wide?

Baijiu: the inputs are grain and packaging, costs are a sliver of the price, and brand plus scarcity hold up the pricing power — fat margin, slow volume; the gross margin is the business model itself. Groceries: the store sells other people’s branded goods at transparent prices in fierce competition, earning hard money through scale and turnover — thin margin, high volume; a low gross margin does not mean a bad business, it means the money in this business is made on “turning fast.”

What this case teaches: margin talk without the business model is empty. Before frowning at a 20% gross margin, ask “how fast does it turn over?” — exactly the question the next lesson’s financial ratios and the DuPont analysis are built to answer.

Key Terms

Revenue Mix
How revenue splits after being broken down by product, region, customer, and other dimensions.
Cost Structure
Each cost’s share of revenue, including variable and fixed costs.
Gross Profit
Revenue minus cost of goods sold; a measure of profitability at the product level.
Period Expenses
Selling, administrative, and R&D expenses of the period, not matched to individual units sold.
Operating Profit
Gross profit minus period expenses; the result of core operations.
Year-over-Year
Compared with the same period a year earlier; the ruler that removes seasonality.
Sequential
Compared with the immediately preceding period; the ruler that reads the latest momentum.
Net Profit Margin
Net income ÷ revenue; the share that finally lands in the pocket.
Key Takeaways
Drill into statements by structure: first revenue scale and growth, then the three profit layers — gross, operating, net — and finally a close look at expense structure; for horizontal comparison, use the year-over-year and sequential rulers together. Gross margin is the fingerprint of a business model — a high or low reading without context is a misreading.

Financial Markets: Mapping the Flow of Money

Money does not move itself: at one end stand savers with money and no projects; at the other, firms with projects and no money. The financial system is the network of pipes joining the two ends. Read this map and you understand why interest rates move everyone — and you can follow one company’s journey from bank loan to IPO.

1What Problem Does the Financial System Solve?

Without a financial system, savers would not know whom to lend to, and firms could never assemble countless small deposits. The system does two jobs: transport — pooling scattered savings into large blocks of capital; and processing information asymmetry — banks screen borrowers for you, while the stock market compresses millions of judgments into a price. The fee: intermediaries collect a “toll” — the interest spread, commissions, management fees.

2Direct vs. Indirect Finance

Two main highways

Indirect finance: savers hand their money to banks, and banks lend it to firms; the two sides never meet. The bank earns the spread, bears the risk, and watches the borrower on the saver’s behalf.

Direct finance: firms issue stocks or bonds and investors put up the money directly (investment banks usually underwrite); investors bear the risk themselves — and save most of the spread.

Banks are good at evaluating opaque small and mid-sized firms; markets are good at pricing transparent large ones. A country’s system is usually a blend of the two.

3Classifying the Markets: A Few Quick Cards

By maturity: instruments of one year or less, solving short-term cash needs, trade in the money market (interbank lending, short-term government bonds, commercial paper); stocks and medium-to-long-term bonds belong to the capital market. By nature, stocks versus bonds is “ownership” versus “creditor’s claim”:

DimensionStockBond
Your roleShareholder: one of the company’s ownersBondholder: the company’s creditor
How you earnDividends and price gains, with no ceilingContracted interest, paid on time and first
RiskHigher, with large swingsRelatively lower, but with default risk
MaturityPerpetual (unless bought back or delisted)A definite maturity date
Priority in bankruptcyPaid lastAhead of shareholders

By vintage: when a security is issued for the first time and the money truly enters the firm, that is the primary market; when securities later change hands among investors, that is the secondary market. The secondary market does not feed the firm directly, but it supplies liquidity — you are only willing to buy because you can always sell.

5Interest Rates: The Price of Money

The “water price” across this pipe network is the interest rate: it balances the totals of saving and investment, and it allocates money among competing uses. Transmission runs both ways — the central bank moves its policy rate, banks’ funding costs and lending rates follow, and firms’ willingness to invest responds in turn (see path ⑤); an overheating economy can also push market rates up. Lesson 1’s discount rate and Lesson 2’s risk-free rate both draw their numbers from this chain — the interest rate is where the formulas of the first two lessons plug in.

6Case: One Company’s Funding Journey, from Loans to IPO

CATL: across the whole map, one node at a time

Take the battery giant CATL as an example (rounded figures from public reporting, illustrative):

  • 2011 · Founding and equity financing The founding team set out on their own with early capital — a startup has no collateral and can only raise money with equity.
  • Growth stage · Bank loans and bonds Expanding orders meant building plants; equipment and order books became the “credit,” and bank loans and bond issues arrived in turn.
  • June 2018 · ChiNext IPO Listed on the Shenzhen Stock Exchange, raising more than ¥5,000,000,000 (primary market); the money entered the company, and early shareholders gained an exit.
  • After listing · A full toolkit Follow-on offerings, bonds, and credit lines on tap — the more mature the company, the larger the share and the lower the cost of direct finance.

The lesson: corporate fundraising climbs a ladder: equity first, then debt, and finally the door of the public markets — it walked every node on this map.

Key Terms

Indirect finance
Funds reach firms through intermediaries such as banks.
Direct finance
Funds reach firms straight through the securities markets.
Money market
The market for short-term funds of one year or less.
Capital market
The market for stocks and medium-to-long-term bonds.
Primary market
Securities’ first issuance; the money enters the firm.
Secondary market
Issued securities change hands among investors.
Initial public offering (IPO)
A company’s first public issuance of stock and listing.
Liquidity
The ability to turn an asset into cash quickly at a fair price.
Key Takeaways

The financial system turns saving into investment: banks run indirect finance, markets run direct finance; the primary market feeds the firm, the secondary market feeds liquidity. The interest rate is the price of money, linking central bank, banks, firms, and households into one chain.

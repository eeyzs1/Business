Strategy · Lesson 1

What Is Strategy: The Art of Trade-offs

On the same route, two equally diligent airlines: one earns money year after year, the other struggles year after year — the gap usually lies not in “who works harder,” but in “who made different choices and dared to give some things up.” This lesson unpacks Michael Porter’s classic answer: strategy = a unique position + trade-offs. By the end you will spot “fake strategy” at a glance: wanting everything, and ending up with nothing.

1Operational Effectiveness Is Not Strategy

A manager’s daily work is doing everything better: faster delivery, fewer defects, leaner budgets. Porter calls this pursuit operational effectiveness. It matters, of course — more value from the same inputs, who wouldn’t want that?

But operational effectiveness has one fatal property: best practices diffuse. Consulting firms, industry conferences, executives changing jobs, vendors’ proposals — all of them tirelessly spread better ways of doing things. So the leading players across an industry all close in on the productivity frontier — the ceiling of capability formed by the best known practices at any moment — while the gaps between them keep shrinking. Porter calls this competitive convergence: everyone grows more and more exhausted, yet nobody makes unusual money, because the gains from efficiency are ultimately handed to customers through lower prices.

Picture the audience in a cinema: the front row stands up, every row behind has to follow, and eventually the whole audience is watching on its feet — more tired than before, still looking at the same screen. Operational effectiveness is standing taller; strategy is switching to a cinema where nobody needs to stand.

2Strategy = A Unique Position + Trade-offs

Concept Card · Porter’s Definition of Strategy

Strategy is positioning — creating a unique value position — and building around it an activity system of mutually reinforcing activities; more important still, strategy must contain trade-offs: deliberately giving up part of the market, part of the service menu, and part of the revenue that looks like free money, so that what remains can be pushed to the extreme and made hard to imitate.

A position can come from different sources: based on product variety (only no-frills transport), based on customer needs (only price-sensitive travelers), or based on access (direct sales and app only, no commissions to intermediaries). Whatever the source, positioning means giving something up — and the part you give up is precisely what sharpens the part you keep.

Why can’t trade-offs be skipped? Two reasons. First, activities conflict: free meals, generous seat pitch, free checked bags, and “the lowest fares in the market” cannot hold at the same time on the cost side; force them together and the result is mediocrity on both ends. Second, the mind holds only one position: there is only one slot for you in the customer’s head, and a brand promise of “both luxurious and cheap” undermines itself — nobody believes it.

3The Three Strategy Questions: Where / How / Sustainability

  1. Where to play: Which markets, which customer groups, what price points, which channels? More important still is the negative list — being explicit about what you will not do.
  2. How to win: Through lower cost or higher value? Which set of activities will make that advantage real and deep?
  3. Sustainability: Why won’t rivals copy you — or why can’t they? Without trade-offs underneath, any position collapses the moment it is copied.

The question most often skipped is the third. Many so-called strategies are really operational targets: double market share within three years — that answers neither “why us,” nor “why us, persistently.”

4Why Trade-offs Make Strategy Hard to Imitate

An imitator’s problem is not “can’t understand it” but “can’t do it without hurting myself.” To copy a low-cost carrier in full, a full-service airline would have to tear down its most profitable first-class cabins, hub-and-spoke network, and frequent-flyer program — imitation would mean self-liquidation. Trade-offs work like a one-way door: light for those already through it, agonizing for those standing in the doorway. A whole chain of interlocking activities defeats point-by-point imitation, while copying the entire system costs a fortune — that is strategy’s true amulet.

Case · Southwest Airlines and Spring Airlines: The Same Grammar of Trade-offs

Southwest Airlines stuck for decades with a “less is more” combination: a single aircraft type (the Boeing 737 family) that makes maintenance, training, and spare parts radically simple; point-to-point routes flying direct into secondary airports, skipping the waits of hub connections; no cabin classes and (historically) no assigned seating, buying ultra-short turnaround times and higher daily aircraft utilization; and no free meals, with every cent saved plowed back into low fares. The combination produced a string of profits spanning decades (illustrative; broken only by the 2020 pandemic), making it one of the most famous strategy cases in business school.

China’s Spring Airlines uses almost exactly the same grammar: a single Airbus A320 fleet, mostly short point-to-point routes, a very small free baggage allowance, meals sold on board, and direct sales channels that cut commission costs — in exchange for fares significantly below full-service peers (an illustrative description of its public model).

The lesson of this case: what imitators cannot copy is not any single item but the consistency of the whole set of trade-offs — the money saved by a single fleet type must keep flowing into low fares and high frequency, not into a half-hearted first class added midway. Add it back out of softness, and the activity system starts fighting itself; the low-cost engine stalls.

Key Terms

Strategy
The set of choices that creates a unique position and consistent trade-offs around it; at its core, deciding what not to do.
Operational Effectiveness
Performing the same activities better than rivals; important, but imitable — it cannot constitute strategy on its own.
Productivity Frontier
The ceiling of capability formed by the best known practices at any given time; the whole industry is pushed toward it.
Competitive Convergence
Rivals grow ever more alike as they copy each other’s best practices; the gains from efficiency are passed to customers through price wars.
Positioning
The unique way a firm chooses to create value; it can stem from variety, needs, or access.
Trade-off
What a firm deliberately gives up to protect its position; unavoidable because activities conflict and the mind holds only one position.
Activity System
The set of mutually reinforcing activities designed around a position; imitating the whole set is prohibitively costly.
Key Takeaways

Strategy is not doing everything well; it is choosing a position and daring to give things up. Operational effectiveness will eventually be copied; a position built on trade-offs is what stays hard to replicate. To judge any strategy, ask three questions first: where to play, how to win, and whether it can hold.

Management · Lesson 4

Teams and Leadership

Put five people in a meeting room: do you get five solo performers, or a team? This lesson covers two things — how teams evolve over time, and why leadership style should never be one-size-fits-all — and closes with a matrix simulator for practicing the art of matching your style to the person.

1From a Group to a Team: Tuckman’s Five Stages

After observing large numbers of small groups, the psychologist Bruce Tuckman proposed in 1965 that virtually all teams pass through four stages in sequence, adding a fifth in 1977. These are Tuckman’s stages, still in use today: forming → storming → norming → performing → adjourning.

The stage most often misread is storming: open conflict looks like bad news, but it is the road a team must travel to become real — disagreements that are never argued out never become rules that are truly kept. The manager’s task is not to eliminate conflict, but to give it rules.

2Two Rulers of Leadership Behavior: Task and Relationship

Any act of leadership can be measured against two rulers. Task behavior: setting what to do, how to do it, and when it must be done — aimed at “moving the work forward.” Relationship behavior: listening, encouraging, supporting, involving people in decisions — aimed at “holding the person.”

Neither ruler ranks above the other; the only question is the mix. Offering a novice nothing but warmth and no method is a gentle form of delay; giving a veteran instructions on every detail is a suffocating form of distrust. What sets the mix? The subordinate’s readiness. That is the starting point of situational leadership.

3Situational Leadership: Style Follows Readiness

Definition · Situational Leadership

In the late 1960s, Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard proposed situational leadership: there is no single best leadership style, only the style that best matches the subordinate’s readiness. Readiness has two dimensions — ability (can they do it?) and willingness (do they want to?) — which combine into four profiles, matched by four styles: directing, coaching, supporting, and delegating.

4Leadership vs Management: Partners, Not Rivals

It is easy to come away from all this with the illusion that “leadership outranks management.” John Kotter’s classic clarification is that the two solve different problems — management copes with complexity and produces order; leadership copes with change and produces movement. Organizations cannot do without either:

ManagementLeadership
Copes withComplexity: making complicated things run reliablyChange: taking people somewhere they have never been
Core movesPlanning and budgeting → organizing and staffing → controlling and problem-solvingSetting direction → aligning people → inspiring and motivating
ProducesThe predictable: order, consistency, on-time deliveryThe hoped-for: change, energy, fresh attempts
When unbalancedManagement only: bureaucracy, rigidity, no one charging forwardLeadership only: rabble-rousing, chaos, no one closing things out

One widely quoted line serves as a memory hook: managers do things right; leaders do the right things. Look back at Lesson 1 — efficiency and effectiveness, the four functions — and they converge again here.

Case: A New Project Team’s First 12 Weeks (Illustrative)

The following illustrative case, synthesized from common project patterns, strings the five stages and situational leadership onto a single timeline. An internet company assembles a five-person cross-functional squad, Qingmiao (literally “green shoots”), with a goal of shipping a new feature in 12 weeks:

  • Weeks 1–2 · Forming — In meetings everyone is polite; nobody volunteers for the gray areas, and everything waits for the lead to make the call. The lead uses directing + coaching: breaking tasks down to individuals, spelling out acceptance criteria, and learning each member’s strengths and worries one by one.
  • Weeks 3–5 · Storming — The technology-choice fight goes public: two key engineers fall out in front of everyone, and one threatens to quit. Instead of papering over it, the lead sets a decision rule — “data decides, and whoever owns it calls it” — and lets the conflict argue itself out within the rules.
  • Weeks 6–8 · Norming — Stand-ups, peer code reviews, and covering for one another become defaults; members start aligning privately instead of escalating everything. The lead shifts to a supporting style: asking “what do you need” more, issuing orders less.
  • Weeks 9–12 · Performing — The team re-prioritizes on its own and ships early (illustrative). The lead moves into delegating: milestone check-ins only, hands off in between.
  • After launch · Adjourning — After the celebration and the retrospective, members scatter to new projects. The lead holds one-on-ones: what capability did you build this time, and where do you want to go next — the team dissolves, but the people stay.

What to notice in this case: leadership style is not the lead’s “personality” but a variable that shifts notch by notch with the team’s stage and each member’s readiness. Same team, same leader: tight in week 2 and loose in week 12 is not inconsistency — the situation changed.

Key Terms

Tuckman’s Stages
The five-stage model of team development: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning.
Task Behavior
Behavior aimed at moving the work forward: assigning goals, demonstrating methods, checking progress.
Relationship Behavior
Behavior aimed at holding the person: listening, encouraging, supporting, involving them in decisions.
Situational Leadership
Hersey and Blanchard’s model: switch among directing, coaching, supporting, and delegating to match subordinate readiness.
Readiness
A subordinate’s combination of ability and willingness on a specific task; it determines the matching leadership style.
Delegation
Shifting decision rights and responsibility to a capable subordinate, agreeing only on goals and checkpoints.
Leadership
The ability to influence others to follow willingly toward a shared goal; it complements management.
Key Takeaways

A team must travel through forming, storming, norming, and performing before it reaches high output; storming is not an accident but the necessary passage. Leadership style switches with each subordinate’s “ability × willingness”: four quadrants, four mixes. Management brings order, leadership brings change — an organization needs both.

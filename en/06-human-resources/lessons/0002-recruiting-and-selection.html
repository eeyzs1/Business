Human Resources · Lesson 2

Recruiting and Selection

Hiring is probably the highest-return decision a manager makes — and the one most easily corrupted by gut feel: forty-five minutes of conversation to judge someone you may work with for years. This lesson turns “reading people” from mysticism into engineering: draw the talent profile accurately first, then use a structured interview with STAR probes to obtain comparable behavioral evidence.

1Hiring Wrong Is the Most Expensive Mistake

In the interview room the candidate has every incentive to “present their best self,” while the interviewer holds only a few hundred seconds of information — the information asymmetry is structural. Worse, the brain ships with several systematic biases: the halo effect (one shining trait beautifies the whole impression — a prestigious degree, so “surely they can grind, too”); the primacy effect (the first five minutes set the tone, and everything after is evidence-gathering); and similar-to-me bias (unconsciously favoring candidates who resemble you). These biases are not attitude problems — they are factory settings. That is why you counter them with process design, not by reminding yourself to “be objective.”

By the way · Résumé screening bias

Name, gender, age, photo, and school can all trigger unconscious associations that tag a résumé before you ever meet its owner. The standard countermeasure is blind screening: mask everything unrelated to competency first, and score only the evidence of ability.

How large is the cost? The visible parts (headhunter fees, probation-period salary) are the small end. The hidden costs of a mismatch — missed opportunities, the drain of repeated team reshaping, the time to hire all over again — are usually far larger (a common rule of thumb puts them at several times the seat’s annual salary; indicative).

2The Talent Profile: From Job Description to Competencies

Concept: Talent Profile and Competencies

Translate the job description (duties and requirements) into a list of competencies: professional knowledge and skills, experience thresholds, and general attributes (structured communication or resilience under pressure, for example). With a vague profile, the widest recruiting funnel just leaks; with a clear profile, interview questions and scoring rubrics finally have a target.

Channels are a trade-off among quality, speed, and cost. Employee referrals deliver high quality at low cost, but guard against an inbred team; job boards deliver volume along with a heavy screening burden; executive search fits scarce, senior roles; campus recruiting buys potential and plasticity. There is no best channel — only a portfolio matched to the role profile.

3The Structured Interview: The Validity Watershed

Concept: Structured Interview

All candidates answer the same set of competency-linked questions and are scored on a shared rating scale (often with behaviorally anchored level descriptions). The opposite — an improvised, ask-whatever-comes-to-mind “chat” — is an unstructured interview.

Why does structure matter so much? Decades of meta-analytic selection research consistently find that structured interviews have significantly higher predictive validity for job performance than unstructured ones (on the order of 0.5 versus 0.4; indicative). The reason is not mysterious — with the same questions and the same standards, scores become comparable, and each interviewer’s personal taste and bias get squeezed out. A side benefit is fairness: every candidate faces the same questions, and should a hiring dispute ever arise, the written scoring record is important procedural evidence.

4Behavioral Interviews and STAR Probing

The most powerful form of structured interviewing is the behavioral interview, and its underlying assumption is a single sentence: past behavior is the best predictor of future performance. So you do not ask “can you” — you ask “have you”; you leave the candidate no room for oratory, and instead drill down layer by layer through one real experience. The map for that drilling is STAR.

5Case Study: Amazon’s Bar Raiser

The Bar Raiser: an interviewer whose job is to push back

Amazon’s hiring process includes a special role: the Bar Raiser (literally, “the one who raises the bar”). This is a specially trained senior interviewer from a department unrelated to the opening, who joins the interviews and the debrief. Their job is not to help the hiring manager “get someone in the seat quickly,” but to hold the company’s long-term bar on people quality — with a voice that can veto unqualified candidates (an illustrative description of the mechanism). When a business unit is desperate for hands, the “hire first, worry later” compromise is most likely to appear; a Bar Raiser who feels none of the delivery pressure is the designed counterweight to that short-termism.

The lesson: systems are more reliable than good intentions. If hiring standards are not to be diluted by “we need someone now,” you cannot count on everyone’s self-discipline — you need a veto designed to sit outside the stakeholders. It is the same idea as “same questions, same scoring sheet” in the structured interview: constrain human nature with process, rather than appeal to it with morality.

Key Terms

Talent Profile
The competency list translated from the job description; the target for question-writing and scoring.
Competency
The observable combination of knowledge, skills, experience, and attributes needed to perform a role.
Structured Interview
An interview with a fixed question set and a shared scoring rubric; comparable scores, higher validity.
Behavioral Interview
A questioning method resting on the assumption that real past behavior predicts future performance.
STAR Method
The probing framework of Situation, Task, Action, and Result.
Validity
How well a selection tool’s scores predict later job performance; expressed as a correlation coefficient (indicative).
Leading Question
A question with almost only one possible answer that yields no real information, such as “are you a detail-oriented person?”
Bar Raiser
Amazon’s interviewer role, independent of the hiring team, charged with holding the bar (illustrative).
Key Takeaways

Accuracy in hiring comes from process, not from an eye for talent: the profile sets the target, structure makes scores comparable, and behavioral interviewing collects the evidence. The soul of STAR is the A — what you did, not how “we” did. An interviewer’s intuition is a hypothesis; behavioral evidence is the test.

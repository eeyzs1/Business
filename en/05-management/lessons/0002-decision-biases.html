Decision-Making: Rationality and Biases

Every move a manager makes is a decision: whom to hire, how much to invest, whether to kill a project. The “rational actor” of the economics textbooks does not exist in the real world — in this lesson you will watch biases take shape inside your own judgment, then learn how to fit your decisions with guardrails.

1Perfect Rationality Is a Myth

The textbook “economic man” lists every alternative, computes every consequence, and picks the optimum. But the Nobel laureate Herbert Simon pointed out that human cognitive capacity and information are both limited, so what real decision makers do is this: set a “good enough” standard in advance, and stop at the first option that clears it. He called this satisficing, as opposed to optimizing, and named the underlying condition bounded rationality.

Definition · Bounded Rationality and Satisficing

Constrained by information, time, and cognitive resources, decision makers do not exhaust every option in search of the optimum; they look within a limited set for a “good enough” satisficing solution. This is not laziness but a sensible strategy under real constraints — the true danger is not failing to optimize, but drifting systematically away from rationality without noticing.

That unnoticed drift is what we call decision biases: not occasional carelessness, but systematic tendencies that everyone shares and whose direction can be predicted. These four appear most often on business ground:

The Bias Lab · Experiment 2: The Loss Frame

Scenario: an unusual disease is expected to kill 600 people. Only two response programs are available, and rigorous statistical projections show:

Program A: 200 people will be saved, guaranteed.
Program B: a 1/3 chance that all 600 will be saved, and a 2/3 chance that no one will be saved.

The same experts report back with different wording — the same disease, the same two programs, but this time stated in terms of “dying”:

Program C: 400 people will die, guaranteed.
Program D: a 1/3 chance that no one will die, and a 2/3 chance that all 600 will die.

3Fitting Decisions with Guardrails

Biases cannot be willed away — people who know all about anchoring still get anchored. What works is changing the process and the environment in which decisions are made:

  • Write the anchor down, then question it: before a negotiation, write down your own target figure and its rationale on your own; flag “where did this number come from.”
  • Assign a devil’s advocate: before every decision, one person is permanently tasked with gathering the counter-evidence — the antidote to confirmation bias.
  • Ask the “zero-based question”: if I were starting from scratch today, carrying no historical investment, would I still choose it? The antidote to sunk costs.
  • Run a premortem: assume the project has failed one year from now, and have the team write down the causes today — an imagined loss forces out the risks that optimism has papered over.

Case: Kodak’s Film Filter, and “Pouring Money into the Hole”

A specimen of confirmation bias: Kodak. In 1975, a Kodak engineer built one of the world’s earliest digital camera prototypes (public record). But for a company whose profits then rested on film, “digital will replace film” was an unwelcome signal. In the years that followed, the company kept its attention on evidence supporting the film business — volumes still growing, the channel still strong — while reading the threat from the substitute technology as “no threat to the core.” By the time the digital wave broke in full, Kodak had filed for bankruptcy protection, in 2012 (public fact). People who saw the threat were not lacking; the bad news was simply filtered out as it climbed the reporting chain — confirmation bias as it looks at the organizational level.

A specimen of sunk costs: “pouring money into the hole.” Business reporting keeps replaying this plot (illustrative): a project that has absorbed a large investment starts to falter, and the review meeting no longer asks “how much more is needed, and what will it return” — instead it argues, over and over, that “one more injection will get us to break-even.” The project fills deeper and deeper, like a hole. It is the same logic by which Britain and France kept funding the Concorde supersonic jet — which is why the sunk-cost fallacy is also called the “Concorde effect.”

What to notice in this case: biases never act alone — confirmation bias supplies “only good news gets through,” sunk costs supply “can’t bear to stop,” and loss aversion supplies “dare not book the loss.” Organizations need counter-evidence forced into the process, rather than counting on the people involved to “stay rational.”

Key Terms

Bounded Rationality
Proposed by Simon: constrained by information and cognition, people make decisions under incomplete conditions.
Satisficing
Stopping the search at the first “good enough” option rather than exhausting all options for the optimum.
Anchoring Bias
An initial number or piece of information disproportionately drags subsequent judgments.
Confirmation Bias
The tendency to seek and weight evidence supporting existing views while ignoring contrary evidence.
Sunk-Cost Fallacy
Letting costs already paid and unrecoverable influence the decision, and escalating the commitment as a result.
Loss Aversion
The psychological pain of a loss is stronger than the satisfaction brought by an equal gain.
Framing Effect
Different wordings of the same information (gain frame vs loss frame) produce opposite choices.
Key Takeaways

It is fine that people seek a satisficing answer rather than the optimum; the problem is being anchored, hearing only good news, and filling holes for sunk costs. Biases cannot be blocked by willpower, but they can be blocked by process: write the anchor down, assign the opposition, ask the zero-based question, run the premortem.

Operations & Supply Chain · Lesson 3

Quality and Lean

The first two lessons solved for “fast”: find the bottleneck, focus your effort there. This lesson solves for “good” and “lean”: should quality rest on inspection or on prevention? And why does Toyota say that nine-tenths of what happens in a factory is waste? The answer is an improvement flywheel you can spin every single day.

1Quality Is Free: The Arithmetic of Prevention

The traditional view treats quality and cost as enemies: want it better, spend more on inspection. The quality guru Philip Crosby argued the opposite in Quality Is Free: quality is free — what is truly expensive is the cost of poor quality. Accountants split the cost of quality into four buckets:

  • Prevention costs: training, process design, mistake-proofing (poka-yoke) — keeping defects from ever happening;
  • Appraisal costs: inspection, testing, sampling — catching defects that have already happened;
  • Internal failure costs: scrap, rework, line stops — the price paid when a defect is caught before it ships;
  • External failure costs: returns, recalls, lawsuits, reputation — the price paid when a defect reaches the customer.

The further down this list the money lands, the more expensive it gets. The 1-10-100 rule of thumb says: a defect that costs ¥1 to prevent at the design stage costs ¥10 to correct in production and ¥100 to remedy once it reaches the market (orders of magnitude, illustrative). So “quality is free” means this: move resources from back-end inspection and firefighting to front-end prevention — spend slightly more up front, failure costs collapse, and the total bill actually shrinks. This echoes Lesson 2: inventory hides defects, and the earlier you intercept them, the cheaper they are.

2PDCA: The Flywheel That Keeps Improvement Turning

How does quality improvement keep sinking in instead of blowing through as a one-off campaign? W. Edwards Deming, the father of statistical quality management, distilled improvement into a cycle that can spin forever — the plan-do-check-act (PDCA) cycle, also called the Deming cycle. It turns “fixed it once” into a mechanism of “one turn a day, the standard one notch higher.”

Hover over or tap any quadrant to see what that step does. Improvement is not one sprint — it is earned lap by lap like this, and the more laps, the higher the standard.
  1. Plan: define the problem, dig for the root cause, and design one small, measurable improvement experiment — the five-whys exercise usually lives here.
  2. Do: run a small-scale pilot while faithfully recording the data; resist the urge to roll it out everywhere at once.
  3. Check: hold the data against expectations: did it work? where did it drift? The most often skipped — and most valuable — step.
  4. Act: standardize what worked into the new norm, and carry what didn’t back to Plan with its lessons — the cycle never stops.

3Lean: Pulling Waste Out by the Roots

On the shop floor of postwar Toyota, Taiichi Ohno arrived at a blunt standard: customers pay only for value-adding activities — changing form, changing place, changing information; everything else is waste (muda in Japanese). He distilled the seven wastes (muda), which became the shared language of lean production:

WasteSpot it in one lineTypical scene on the floor
① OverproductionMaking more, earlier, and faster than the next step needsFinished goods filling the warehouse (the worst of the worst)
② WaitingPeople wait for parts, machines wait for people, jobs wait for batchesLong stretches with nothing to do at a station
③ TransportMaterials take long trips around the plant without gaining valueForklifts crossing the entire shop floor
④ OverprocessingMore precision, more steps than the customer asked forPolishing an inconsequential surface twice
⑤ InventoryAny stay longer than one step requiresRaw material and work-in-process stacked layer on layer
⑥ MotionBending, turning, rummaging — human movement that adds no valueTools kept in a cabinet three meters away
⑦ DefectsRework, scrap, recallsThe rework bench never has an empty seat

Two paired mechanisms power the hunt for waste. The first is just-in-time (JIT): make and move only what is needed, when it is needed, in the quantity needed — using kanban as the pull signal by which each downstream step withdraws parts from the step before, so that overproduction becomes physically impossible. The second is kaizen: continuous improvement by everyone, every day, in small steps — PDCA in its everyday shop-floor form. As for Six Sigma, it is the statistical quality management method that General Electric and others championed around the turn of the millennium: five DMAIC steps driving the defect rate toward a target of 3.4 defects per million — the same lineage of thinking as PDCA, with heavier statistical tools.

A word of cautionLean is not a reckless dash to zero inventory: without stable quality and reliable suppliers, slashing inventory only snaps the line. Lean is “stabilize first, then accelerate” — first use quality to squeeze out variation, then peel away inventory, the shock-absorbing cushion, layer by layer.

5Case: A Cord That Can Stop the Whole Line

Toyota’s Andon Cord

On Toyota’s final assembly line, an andon cord hangs above every workstation. Any frontline worker who spots an abnormality at their station — a missing part, a wrong assembly, a torque out of range — can pull it at once: the andon board overhead lights up, a chime sounds, and the team leader arrives within the takt time; if the problem is not solved in time, the entire line stops. On a line that produces roughly one car per minute (illustrative magnitude), every stopped minute is visible money — yet Toyota hands the power to halt the line to the workers on the lowest rung.

The logic is exactly where this lesson’s two main threads converge. First, quality must be intercepted at the source — a defect that flows to the next station doubles the rework bill, and one that reaches the customer becomes a recall (1-10-100). Second, a line stop is the loudest improvement alarm: every andon pull is the P of a PDCA cycle, exposing and zeroing out the problem on the spot instead of letting it hide overnight under layers of inventory.

The operations lesson: give the power — and the incentive — to spot problems to the people closest to them, and trade small line stops for giant recalls. This is the exact opposite of many factories’ “the line must never stop; problems can wait until after the shift,” and that latter approach is precisely the hothouse where defects and inventory cover for each other. Key Terms

PDCA Cycle
Plan-Do-Check-Act, the Deming cycle: the plan → do → check → act loop of continuous improvement.
Cost of Quality
The four cost buckets — prevention, appraisal, internal failure, external failure; the further down the list, the more expensive.
Seven Wastes (Muda)
Overproduction, waiting, transport, overprocessing, inventory, motion, defects — every consumption the customer will not pay for.
Just-in-Time (JIT)
Produce and move only what is needed, when it is needed, in the quantity needed; pull signals replace push scheduling.
Kanban
The visual card that carries withdrawal and production instructions from downstream to upstream steps — the signal carrier of a pull system.
Kaizen
Continuous improvement by everyone, in small daily steps — PDCA’s shop-floor form.
Andon
A visual abnormality-alert system; the andon cord gives frontline workers the authority to stop the line.
Six Sigma
A quality management method that uses statistical tools (DMAIC) to drive the defect rate toward a target of 3.4 per million.
Key Takeaways
Quality lives not in the inspection room but at the source, in prevention — the farther a defect travels, the bigger the bill. Lean treats every activity the customer will not pay for as waste: JIT strangles overproduction, and kaizen spins PDCA once a day. Remember: inventory is the cover-up that hides problems; the andon cord is the searchlight that exposes them.

Further Reading

  • Taiichi Ohno, Toyota Production System (in Chinese, China Railway Publishing House; read: the chapters on kanban and just-in-time) — the first-hand source of the seven wastes and JIT; brief, and all but a manifesto.
  • Lean Thinking by James Womack & Daniel Jones (read: the value and value-stream chapters) — distills the Toyota experience into five principles: value → value stream → flow → pull → perfection.
  • Quality Is Free by Philip Crosby (read: the chapters on the cost of quality and prevention) — the primary source for this lesson’s first section; also worth searching for: W. Edwards Deming’s Out of the Crisis.

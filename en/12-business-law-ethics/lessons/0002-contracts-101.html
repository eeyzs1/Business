Contracts: The Glue of Business

Every business is a pile of promises: what gets delivered, at what price, by when, and what happens when things go wrong. A contract is the technology that turns promises into enforceable agreements — it costs little, yet it decides whether you hold any cards when the relationship turns sour. In this lesson you learn how contracts are formed and how to review one, then hunt for landmines yourself in a rigged purchase agreement.

Read this first This lesson and its flaw-hunting exercise are teaching illustrations and do not constitute legal advice; the contract text is fictional. For significant transactions, have a licensed attorney draft and review the documents.

1How a contract is formed: offer, acceptance, and consideration

A contract does not need a red seal stamped on it to “count” — its skeleton is just two moves. An offer is a manifestation of intent by one party, specific and definite in content, showing an intent to be bound once it is accepted: a purchase order naming the goods, the quantity, and the price is an offer. Goods displayed at marked prices in a shop window, or a product page in an online store, are usually treated as an invitation to offer — an invitation for you to make the offer. When the other side accepts unconditionally, that is acceptance: when acceptance takes effect, the contract is in principle formed. A reply saying “we agree to all terms, shipping next week” is acceptance; a reply saying “the price works, but payment becomes net 60 after delivery” is a new offer — negotiation is just passing the ball back and forth between offers and counteroffers.

Definition · Consideration, and why promises get enforced

Consideration is a common-law concept: each side gives up something — money for goods, services for commission — and that exchange is precisely the economic reason the law protects a contract. Civil-law systems (including Chinese law) do not use the term, but the spirit is the same: rights and obligations must correspond; free-standing promises and one-sided favors are usually not treated as contracts. Once you see this, you understand: contract law does not protect the virtue of keeping one’s word — it protects a transaction structure that can be relied upon. It is precisely because promises can be enforced that strangers dare to ship first and collect later.

Let us correct a common misconception along the way: verbal agreements can, as a rule, form valid contracts (except where the law requires writing — illustrative). The problem is never “invalidity” but the burden of proof: once you are in court, what was never written down might as well never have existed. So “put it in writing” is mostly about preserving evidence, setting expectations, and sparing yourselves the argument.

2The life of a contract: from negotiation to remedies for breach

  • Negotiate: Probe, quote, haggle — usually no contract yet, but bad-faith bargaining or leaking secrets can still create pre-contractual liability (illustrative).
  • Draft: Write the agreed terms into clauses; whoever holds the pen sets the tone first — wording is interest.
  • Sign: Verify the parties and their authority: seals, the signatory’s identity, the date — assume nothing.
  • Perform: Deliver, inspect, invoice, pay; every letter, site sign-off, and chat log during performance is one link in the chain of evidence.
  • Amend: Change quantities, extend deadlines, swap specifications — lock changes into a written supplemental agreement; a verbal amendment sends you straight back to the proof problem.
  • Remedies for breach: Demand performance, remedial measures, damages, or the agreed liquidated damages; the remedy clause is the contract’s “teeth.”

3The five-point review: reading a contract like a lawyer

Whatever contract lands on your desk, professionals run it through these five gates first, and the order matters — from “who am I dealing with” to “what happens when it breaks”:

The five checksWhat to look atCommon landmines
Check 1 · Parties and authorityIs the counterparty a legal person or organization with the power to contract? Is the signatory authorized?Signing with an internal unit such as a “project department” or “preparation office”; the seal and the name do not match
Check 2 · Subject matter and qualityWhat is delivered, how much, to what standard, inspected how?A product name with no standard; no inspection deadline or objection window
Check 3 · Price and paymentIs the unit price locked? Tax included or not? What payment milestones, and how many days?“Settled at market price”; “pay after acceptance” with no day count
Check 4 · Liability for breachWhat are the penalties for delay, quality failures, leaks?Nothing but “friendly negotiation” — breach costs nothing
Check 5 · Dispute resolutionWhich court has jurisdiction, or which arbitration institution? Which law applies?“Resolved according to law” — no venue named, so the first fight after a dispute is over jurisdiction

The first four checks decide whether you make or lose money; the fifth decides where the fight happens when things go bad, how long it lasts, and how much it costs. Many businesspeople skip the fifth check, and only discover — when payment never arrives — that the counterparty’s court is two thousand kilometers away (illustrative).

Case: The typical path by which a verbal “buddy deal” crashes (illustrative)

Business disputes include one highly repetitive script, whose leads are usually old classmates or army buddies (a typical path, illustrative — no specific company):

  • Launched over drinks: “Let’s do this together — 60/40 if we make money.” No written agreement; pure friendship.
  • Honeymoon: The business runs on trust; transfer memos are a mess — “loan,” “stock purchase,” and “dividend” all flow through one account.
  • Cracks: One side puts in equipment and patents, the other holds the customer relationships — and each keeps a different ledger on how contributions should count.
  • The market turns: Business sours, the splits stop; the chat log becomes the only “contract.”
  • Court: An oral partnership must be reconstructed from bank records and witness testimony — proof is brutally hard, and cases run for years (illustrative).
  • Hindsight: A modestly priced written agreement could have locked down ninety percent of the disputes in advance (illustrative).

What to notice in this case: the point of a contract is not “preparing for litigation” — it is not going to court at all. Writing the hard words down first is precisely how you protect the relationship: the terms hardest to say to a friend’s face, a document says for both of you.

Key Terms

Offer
A manifestation of intent, specific and definite in content, showing the offerer is bound upon acceptance — for example, a purchase order naming goods, quantity, and price.
Invitation to offer
An invitation for the other party to make an offer — price lists, shop-window displays, tender announcements.
Acceptance
The offeree’s unqualified acceptance of the offer; when acceptance takes effect, the contract is in principle formed.
Consideration
A common-law concept: the value each side gives up; the exchange is what makes a contract enforceable.
Liquidated damages
A breach-compensation amount or formula agreed in advance, putting a definite price on breach.
Jurisdiction
The agreed court that will hear disputes; absent an agreement, statutory rules apply — and fights over jurisdiction follow.
Arbitration
Referring disputes to an arbitration institution for a final, binding award — usually faster and private, but it requires an arbitration agreement.
Burden of proof
Whoever asserts a fact must prove it; verbal deals are dangerous precisely because they are hard to prove.
Key Takeaways

Offer plus acceptance, and the contract stands; whether it can be enforced — and enforced without a fight — depends on clauses written in advance. Run the five checks in order: parties, subject matter, price, breach, disputes. A contract is not there to guard against the other side; it guards against yourself when the relationship turns.

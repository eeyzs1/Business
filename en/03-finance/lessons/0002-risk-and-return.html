Risk and Return

In the world of investing there are only two currencies: return and risk. A pitch that quotes the return and never mentions the risk is like quoting a price without the weight. Learning to read both numbers at once — and to buy them in combination — is the real entry ticket.

1Return and Risk: Two Sides of the Same Coin

Return measures how much you made: (ending value − initial investment + dividends along the way) ÷ initial investment. And the most common measure of “risk” in finance is volatility — the standard deviation of returns. It answers not “how much can I lose” but “how badly behaved are the outcomes”: the bigger the volatility, the wider the gap between the best and the worst years.

A quick intuition

Never compare two funds on average return alone. A product averaging 8% with 4% volatility and one averaging 8% with 25% volatility can compound to very different long-run results — the second has to climb out of a deep hole before its snowball can roll again.

2The Risk Premium: What the Market Pays for Volatility

Over the long run, stocks as a whole have returned more than government bonds. The excess is the risk premium: the market’s payment to those willing to bear volatility. The starting point is the risk-free rate, usually approximated by the short-term government-bond rate. Remember this food chain: the risk-free rate is the floor, and each asset class marks up from it according to its volatility. Risk that can be cheaply eliminated earns no premium from anyone — which brings us to this lesson’s protagonist.

3Diversification: The Only Free Lunch

Combine stocks and bonds with weights w and (1−w): the portfolio’s expected return is the weighted average of the two, but its volatility depends on the correlation coefficient (ρ):

σp2 = w2σS2 + (1−w)2σB2 + 2w(1−w)ρσSσB

As long as ρ < 1 (the two assets are not perfect copies rising and falling in lockstep), portfolio risk is below the weighted average of risks, while expected return is not discounted at all. The volatility that disappears costs no return — which is why Markowitz called diversification “the only free lunch.”

4Systematic vs. Unsystematic Risk

What diversification can eliminate is only unsystematic risk: problems specific to one company — accounting fraud, a fire, a failed flagship product. Rate hikes, recessions, and geopolitical shocks hit every asset: that is systematic risk, and no amount of diversification helps against it. 5Case: Why Index Funds Beat Most Stock Pickers Over the Long Run

The fund that picks nothing

In 1976, John Bogle launched the first index fund for ordinary investors at Vanguard, buying the whole basket of the market with almost no active selection. Ridiculed at the time as “a choice for the mediocre,” index funds are now among the largest investment vehicles in the world.

Two pieces of oft-cited evidence (illustrative): Buffett’s “ten-year bet” — he wagered on an S&P 500 index fund that, after ten years, beat a basket of funds picked by hedge-fund managers; and S&P’s annual SPIVA reports — over roughly fifteen-year horizons, most active stock funds trail their own benchmarks.

The reason is no mystery: the information of millions of investors is already “stirred into” prices, and active stock picking still pays research and trading costs; index funds, with near-complete diversification and rock-bottom costs, reliably capture the market’s return. Most people lose to fees and luck, not to the index’s cleverness.

The lesson: diversification removes single-stock risk, fees erode compounding, and only systematic risk earns a premium — this lesson’s three ideas come together in a single product: the index fund.

Key Terms

Return
The ratio of gain to amount invested, including dividends and price change.
Volatility
The standard deviation of returns; measures how uncertain the outcome is.
Risk-free rate
The starting point of returns, approximated by the short-term government-bond rate.
Risk premium
Compensation above the risk-free rate for bearing risk.
Correlation coefficient (ρ)
How closely two assets rise and fall together; ranges from −1 to 1.
Diversification
Combining multiple assets to reduce unsystematic risk.
Systematic risk
Risk shared by the entire market; cannot be diversified away.
Unsystematic risk
Risk specific to a single company; can be diversified away.
Key Takeaways

Risk is not just “the chance of losing money” — it is the volatility of outcomes, measured as the standard deviation of returns. Return must always be quoted together with risk: only systematic risk earns a premium. Diversification is the only free lunch — it shaves away single-stock risk without sacrificing expected return.

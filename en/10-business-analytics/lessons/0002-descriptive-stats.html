Descriptive Statistics: Don’t Be Fooled by Averages

A job posting boasts an “average monthly salary of ¥25,000”; once you join, you find half the staff earn under ¥15,000 — nobody lied, the mean is simply speaking on behalf of the outliers. When Lesson 1’s five analytics steps pull into the “analyze” station, the first task is to summarize a mountain of data with a few well-chosen numbers. This lesson drills three fundamentals so numbers cannot fool you: telling the mean from the median, sensing how spread out data is, and picking the right chart.

1Mean vs Median: Which One Gets Hijacked by Outliers

Concept Card · Mean and Median

The mean = sum ÷ count; every value participates in the calculation, so it is extremely sensitive to outliers. The median = the middle value after sorting; it looks only at position, not magnitude, so it is robust. Income, home prices, and order values come from right-skewed distributions whose long tail is dragged out by a few huge values — the mean is often higher than the median. A few very high incomes can raise the mean, but that does not imply that every individual earns less than it.

2The Intuition of Spread: Variance and Standard Deviation

Knowing “where the middle is” is not enough; you also need “how scattered the data is”. Variance averages the squared distances of every point from the mean; its square root — the Standard Deviation (SD) — is in the same units as the original data, which makes it far easier to interpret: the larger the SD, the more scattered the data, and the harder it is to predict “the next customer who walks in”. Imagine two stores that both average 800 visits a day: one sits between 780 and 820 every single day, the other swings between 300 and 1,300 (illustrative) — identical means, yet stocking and staffing strategies worlds apart. The mean tells you where to go; the standard deviation tells you how dangerous the road is.

3The Aggregation Trap: Simpson’s Paradox

One-Line Warning · Simpson’s Paradox
Merging grouped data into one total can flip the conclusion: an app’s paying conversion rate can be lower than a competitor’s on both iOS and Android separately, yet higher once pooled (a classic teaching example). Different user mixes give the groups different weights. Inspect both views and first decide whether you want overall performance or performance under comparable conditions. For a causal question, also check whether the grouping variable is a confounder; neither table automatically takes priority.

Picking the right chart is not enough — you also have to keep charts from “lying”. The most common trick is the Truncated Axis: raising the y-axis baseline above 0 blows a tiny difference up into a dramatic cliff:

Case · Tech-Giant Employees “Earning a Million a Year on Average”?

Every earnings season produces headlines like this one: divide Tencent Holdings’ staff-related expenses in its annual report by headcount, and you get “an average annual salary of about ¥800,000–1,000,000” (a public-report figure, illustrative). The arithmetic is not wrong — the definition is: adding executive pay and share-based incentives to the salaries of a hundred-odd thousand ordinary employees and dividing by headcount yields the mean; and because the pay distribution is heavily right-skewed, the mean is necessarily pulled up by the top, far above the median — “the number half the employees never reach”. It is also why statistics agencies and hiring platforms increasingly stress publishing a “median wage” figure (illustrative). Before you trust any statistic, ask three questions: What definition is this? Mean or median? Are there outliers in the data?

The lesson: for right-skewed data (income, home prices, durations), look at the median first; treat “per-capita” headlines as probably lifted by outliers. The number did not lie — the summary method was speaking for someone.

Key Terms

Mean
Sum divided by count; every value participates, extremely sensitive to outliers.
Median
The middle value after sorting; position only, robust to outliers.
Outlier
A few observations far from the rest; they can hijack the mean but cannot move the median.
Standard Deviation
The square root of the variance; measures spread around the mean in the data’s own units.
Right Skew
A distribution with a long right tail dragged out by a few huge values; the mean exceeds the median.
Simpson’s Paradox
An association reverses direction between grouped and pooled data; interpretation requires group weights and causal context.
Truncated Axis
A y-axis that does not start at 0; visually magnifies small differences — a common misleading device.
Key Takeaways

For right-skewed data, look at the median first, and read spread with the standard deviation; choose charts by purpose — lines for trends, bars for comparisons, pie charts (sparingly) for composition, scatter plots for relationships; when a chart looks shocking, check the axis baseline first. These three moves are the security gate for the next lesson, “correlation is not causation”.

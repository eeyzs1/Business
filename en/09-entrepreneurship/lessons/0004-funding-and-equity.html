Entrepreneurship · Lesson 4

Funding and Equity

With validation concluded and the plan written down, the final lesson talks about money: where it comes from, and what you pay with. Equity financing sells the future — this lesson works through the funding ladder and the arithmetic of dilution, so that before you ever sit down with an investor, you know the true price of every dollar.

1Equity Is the Most Expensive Money

Founders often treat “closing a round” as an achievement, but it is really just a different way of paying dearly: a loan repays interest, while equity gives up a claim on future dividends and votes. The 15% you give up at the angel round, if the company eventually reaches ¥10 billion, is ¥1.5 billion. So the first principle of fundraising is not “take all you can get,” but: every round of money must buy a clear milestone — otherwise you are better off not raising at all.

2The Funding Ladder: From 3F to IPO

  • Own savings and the 3F round (Founder, Family, Friends) — the idea-validation stage, on the order of ¥100,000 to ¥1,000,000 (illustrative). The gentlest terms, but the highest human cost: if the venture falls apart, the people most easily hurt are this round’s backers.
  • Angel investors — betting on the person and the product prototype, on the order of ¥1,000,000-plus (illustrative), typically for about 10%–20% of equity (illustrative). Mostly individual money, quick decisions, and early resources attached.
  • Venture capital (VC), Series A/B/C — the acceleration phase after product–market fit is proven, on the order of ¥10 million to ¥100 million (illustrative), giving up about 15%–25% per round (illustrative). Institutional funds, complex terms, hunting for multi-x returns at exit.
  • Private equity (PE) and the growth stage — expansion, acquisitions, or restructuring of mature businesses, valuing the certainty of cash flows over their mere possibility.
  • Initial public offering (IPO) — listing on the public market, giving early shareholders and investors a liquid exit channel, while accepting the strictest information disclosure.

3The Arithmetic of Dilution: One Division

New investor’s share = amount raised ÷ post-money valuation

Post-money valuation means the company’s total valuation after this round’s money arrives. For example, an angel round of ¥5,000,000 at a post-money valuation of ¥40,000,000 gives the new investor 5,000,000 ÷ 40,000,000 = 12.5%; that 12.5% does not appear out of thin air — every existing shareholder cedes it proportionally. A founder who held 100% is left with 87.5%. Every later round repeats the same division, and the stake shrinks multiplicatively, round after round.

5Beyond Dilution: Keeping Control

Being diluted down to 30–40% does not necessarily mean losing control, because equity ≠ voting rights. Two common safeguards: dual-class shares — the founder’s Class B shares carry several times the votes of ordinary shares, a structure used by Google and Meta as well as JD.com, Xiaomi, and many other tech companies (publicly disclosed facts; the multiples vary); and voting agreements — several founding shareholders pledge to vote as a bloc, bundling scattered votes into one bundle. Beyond these, board seats, veto rights over major decisions, and similar terms also decide “who calls the shots.” All this lesson asks you to remember: stakes are arithmetic; control is design.

Case Study · One Company’s Four Rounds, Played Out (Purely Illustrative)

Imagine a fictional company running the “typical rhythm” through four rounds: the founder starts at 100%, first sets aside a 10% employee option pool, and each round thereafter gives up “amount raised ÷ post-money valuation”:

After four rounds, the founder has been diluted from 100% to about 38% — the textbook range (roughly 30%–40%). The key is how you read it: 38% of a ¥1 billion company dwarfs 100% of zero. If every round genuinely bought a growth milestone, dilution is an amplifier; only when the money runs out and the milestone never arrives does dilution become a pure loss.

The lesson: dilution itself is not the danger; the dangers are “accepting harsh terms for an inflated valuation” and “giving up so much that nothing is left to give and motivation collapses.” Keep each round’s give-up within a sensible range, and use devices such as dual-class shares to hold on to control.

Key Terms

3F Round (Founder / Family / Friends)
The earliest funding: personal savings plus money from relatives and friends; gentle terms, but high cost in relationships.
Angel Investment
Early investment by individuals at the product-prototype stage, typically for about 10%–20% of equity (illustrative).
Venture Capital
Growth-stage equity investment from institutional funds chasing high-multiple exits.
Post-money Valuation
The company’s total valuation after this round’s money arrives; equals pre-money valuation plus the amount raised.
Dilution
The proportional fall in existing shareholders’ stakes as new shareholders come in.
Option Pool
Shares reserved for future employees, usually set up before a round and ceded by existing shareholders.
Dual-class Share
A structure of unequal voting rights: the founder’s shares carry more votes per share than ordinary stock, preserving control with a smaller stake.
Key Takeaways

New investor’s share = amount raised ÷ post-money valuation, with existing shareholders diluted proportionally; falling from 100% to 30–40% is normal, provided each round buys a milestone; stakes are arithmetic, and control is design.

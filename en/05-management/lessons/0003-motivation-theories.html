Motivation: Why People Are Willing to Work Hard

Same role, same pay — one person volunteers to push the work to excellence, while another just wants to coast no matter what you pay. Motivation is at once the most mysterious and the most valuable link in management — this lesson takes three classic theories apart so you can see on which gear money, recognition, and growth each do their work.

1Maslow: Needs Come in Layers

The psychologist Abraham Maslow proposed the hierarchy of needs: human needs are stacked in layers like a pyramid, and only after a lower layer has been roughly satisfied does the next layer become the dominant driver of behavior. In the workplace, every layer has its own “management language”:

Two cautions when using it. First, the hierarchy is about which need dominates, not a strict sequence — some people on good incomes still hunger for belonging, and some people do their most creative work amid turmoil. Second, it reminds managers to diagnose “what is this person missing right now”: preaching mission to someone afraid of layoffs, or offering nothing but bonuses to someone hungry for growth, is prescribing the wrong medicine.

2Herzberg: Satisfaction and Dissatisfaction Are Not the Same Thing

Frederick Herzberg discovered a counterintuitive fact: the factors that remove employees’ dissatisfaction and the factors that generate their engagement are two different sets of things. This is the two-factor theory:

Definition · The Two Factors

Hygiene factors: pay, company policies, working conditions, interpersonal relations, security. Their absence causes dissatisfaction, but supplying them only gets you to “no dissatisfaction” — they do not ignite enthusiasm. They are like painkillers: they neither cure the illness nor pep you up.
Motivators: a sense of achievement, recognition, the meaning of the work itself, responsibility, advancement and growth. These are what actually drive engagement — like an igniter, what they light is the “I want to.”

The one-line version: a raise treats the illness; achievement lights the fire. That is why the familiar plot after a raise is that complaints disappear while engagement does not rise. The sorting game below tests whether you can keep the two buckets straight:

      3Expectancy Theory: Three Links Multiplied — Break One and You Get Zero

      Victor Vroom’s expectancy theory decomposes “willingness to exert effort” into a multiplication chain — note that it multiplies rather than adds, so if any link is zero, the result is zero:

      Motivational force M = Expectancy E × Instrumentality I × Valence V
      • Expectancy (E): effort → performance. “If I go all out, can I hit the target?” If the goal is set too high or resources are too thin, the answer is zero.
      • Instrumentality (I): performance → reward. “If I do hit the target, will the reward actually be delivered to me?” If appraisal and incentives are decoupled, the answer is zero.
      • Valence (V): reward → I care. “Is the reward on offer the one I want?” Pay-only for someone seeking growth, or vision-only for someone feeding a family, and the answer is zero.

      4Intrinsic Motivation: Beware the Side Effects of Rewards

      There is one more force that runs on no external reward: intrinsic motivation — doing a thing for its own sake, out of curiosity, enjoyment, or meaning. It does not simply stack on top of external rewards. In a classic 1973 study by the psychologist Mark Lepper and colleagues (illustrative), children who already loved drawing were given material rewards for drawing; once the rewards stopped, they spent less time drawing than children who had never been rewarded — the external reason crowded out the internal one. This is the overjustification effect.

      Management tip Reward behavior and growth (recognize progress, offer a bigger stage); be wary of using rewards to buy what people already love. This goes double for creative work — underpaying fails, but money alone cannot purchase passion.

      Case: Haidilao’s Front-Line Comping Authority (Illustrative)

      Published sources such as the book Haidilao You Xue Bu Hui — literally “You Can’t Learn Haidilao” (in Chinese) — describe one detail (illustrative): Haidilao’s front-line servers hold limited authority to waive a bill or comp a dish — when a customer is unhappy, they can decide on the spot, without escalating layer by layer. By conventional logic, a waived bill is a “loss,” and handing that authority to the lowest level looks like risk; Haidilao’s logic is the opposite: put service decisions in the hands of the people closest to the customer.

      Seen through this lesson’s lenses, the move hits three theoretical points at once. For front-line staff, trust and the right to make the call are motivators (responsibility and recognition — they ignite the “this is my restaurant” kind of engagement), not mere hygiene conditions. For customers, recovery requires no waiting; the experience loop closes on the spot. For the organization, pushing decisions downward buys service speed. Against the many chains where “servers have no authority and everything escalates,” the contrast shows exactly this: the key to motivation design often lies not in the payroll table but in the structure of authority and accountability.

      What to notice in this case: comping authority is, in essence, swapping “hygiene-style process control” for “motivator-style grants of trust.” It also marks the boundary — delegation must be paired with training and review, or trust turns into loss of control (situational leadership in Lesson 4 picks up exactly this topic).

      Key Terms

      Motivation
      The internal psychological process that arouses behavior, sustains it, and directs it toward a goal.
      Hierarchy of Needs
      Proposed by Maslow: five levels — physiological, safety, social, esteem, and self-actualization — take turns dominating behavior.
      Hygiene Factors
      One of Herzberg’s two factors: their absence causes dissatisfaction; supplying them only reaches “no dissatisfaction.”
      Motivators
      The second of the two factors: achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth — what truly drive engagement.
      Expectancy Theory
      Proposed by Vroom: motivational force = expectancy × instrumentality × valence, a product of three links.
      Instrumentality
      The perceived probability that performance will be rewarded — “if I deliver, will the organization honor it?”
      Valence
      The value a reward holds for the individual — “do I care about what is on offer?”
      Overjustification Effect
      External rewards crowd out internal reasons, and the original interest declines as a result.
      Key Takeaways

      Hygiene factors treat the illness; motivators light the fire — a raise removes dissatisfaction, achievement ignites engagement. Expectancy theory multiplies three links: effort can reach the target, the target gets rewarded, and the reward is one I care about — break any link and motivation falls to zero.

      Further Reading

      • Organizational Behavior by Stephen P. Robbins & Timothy A. Judge (16th edition) — read the motivation chapters for the textbook-grade treatment of all three theories.
      • Motivation and Personality by Abraham H. Maslow — the original statement of the hierarchy of needs; read it for the concept of the “dominant” need.
      • The Practice of Management by Peter F. Drucker — read the chapter “Management by Objectives and Self-Control” to connect motivation to goals.

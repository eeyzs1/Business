The Accounting Equation: The Language of Business

Accounting is often called the language of business, and the first sentence of that language is only one line long: Assets = Liabilities + Owners’ Equity. In this lesson we will not memorize a chart of accounts — we will build a single intuition instead: every transaction has two sides at once, and the scale always stays level.

1Why Start with One Equation

Face any company in the world, and the business world is always asking three questions: What does it own? What does it owe? What is truly left for the shareholders? Banks ask before lending, investors ask before buying in, and owners ask themselves at every month-end. The accounting equation compresses those three questions into a one-line identity — it is not a computing formula but a constitution that always holds.

Accounting Equation

Assets = Liabilities + Owners’ Equity

Assets are the economic resources a company controls: cash, inventory, equipment, the amounts customers still owe. Liabilities are what it owes others: bank loans, unpaid supplier bills. Owners’ equity is the residual left when liabilities are subtracted from assets — also called net assets. Equity behaves like a balance, passively pushed around by both sides of the equation — settle the assets and liabilities first, and equity falls out on its own.

Synonym cheat sheet Owners’ equity = shareholders’ equity = net assets = book value — four names for the same thing, used interchangeably in exams and annual reports.

2The Accounting Entity: Drawing a Boundary Around “the Company”

Before you can record anything with the equation, you must answer one question: whose books are these? The accounting entity defines the boundary of the books: it is this company — not the owner as a person, and not the family’s private savings. Every yuan on the company’s books must be traceable to a transaction at the level of the company.

Why the fuss? Because the most common disease of young startups is commingling: the founder’s personal card pays company costs, and company money pays for family holidays. Once the two mix, the statements lose their meaning — you can no longer tell whether the company actually made money, and fundraising due diligence and tax filing both sink into the mud.

Watch out When an owner pays company costs out of their own pocket and keeps the receipts, that is perfectly proper — a shareholder lending to the company. What goes wrong is not where the money comes from; it is where the books draw the line.

3Double-Entry Bookkeeping: Every Transaction Has Two Sides

The equation’s most beautiful corollary: no transaction touches only one place. Every transaction either raises or lowers both sides of the equation together, or swaps one up and one down within the same side — that is double-entry bookkeeping. Written in the language of the ledger, it becomes debit-and-credit bookkeeping: a debit and a credit are not the everyday “borrowing and lending” — they are simply left and right position markers, summed up by the motto “every debit has a credit, and debits must equal credits.” The journal is the book that records these two-sided entries one by one, in chronological order.

5Case Study: The Lemonade Stand’s First Week

The first week’s journal of one lemonade stand (figures are illustrative)

The Accounting Game teaches an entire set of books through a lemonade stand; let us replay it here. Suppose you decide to start a business selling lemonade:

  • Day 1 · You invest ¥200. You put your own savings into the stand: debit “Cash” ¥200, credit “Paid-in Capital” ¥200 — assets and equity rise together, and from this moment the right-hand pan carries your share.
  • Day 1 · You borrow ¥100 from family. Debit “Cash” ¥100, credit “Loans” ¥100 — both pans gain again, but what the right pan gains is a liability: this part must be repaid later.
  • Day 2 · You buy supplies for cash ¥80. Lemons and sugar not yet sold are still your assets: debit “Inventory” ¥80, credit “Cash” ¥80 — assets merely change shape, and the total does not move.
  • Day 3 · Sales of ¥150, using ¥60 of supplies. Two entries: debit “Cash” ¥150 and credit “Revenue” ¥150; debit “Cost of Sales” ¥60 and credit “Inventory” ¥60. The ¥90 difference between revenue and cost rolls into “Retained Earnings” within equity at the weekend.
  • Day 5 · You repay ¥50 of the loan. Debit “Loans” ¥50, credit “Cash” ¥50 — both sides shrink, and the liability thins with every repayment.

Weekend count-up: cash ¥320 (200 + 100 − 80 + 150 − 50) + inventory ¥20 = assets ¥340; liabilities are down to the ¥50 loan; equity = paid-in capital ¥200 + this week’s profit ¥90 = ¥290. ¥340 = ¥50 + ¥290 — the scale closes.

What this case teaches: “earned ¥90” and “cash went up” are two different things — profit is computed as revenue minus costs, while cash is merely one form of asset. That crack is exactly the problem the next lesson’s three financial statements deal with formally.

Key Terms

Accounting Equation
Assets = Liabilities + Owners’ Equity; an identity that always holds.
Assets
The economic resources a company controls: cash, inventory, equipment, receivables, and more.
Liabilities
Obligations owed to outsiders: borrowings, payables, and the like.
Owners’ Equity
What remains of assets after liabilities; it belongs to the shareholders and is also called net assets.
Accounting Entity
The boundary of the books: record the accounts of “this company,” not the owner’s personal affairs.
Double-Entry Bookkeeping
Every transaction is recorded in two places at once, keeping the equation balanced.
Debit / Credit
Left and right position markers for recording entries — not everyday borrowing and lending.
Journal
The original book that records transactions one by one in chronological order.
Key Takeaways
Assets = Liabilities + Owners’ Equity is a constitution that cannot be violated. Every transaction strikes both sides of the equation at once (or two places on the same side), so the scale always stays level — not by coincidence, but by the very design of double-entry bookkeeping.

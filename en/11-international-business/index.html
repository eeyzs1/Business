Branch 11 · International Business

International Business

The moment a business crosses a border, it plays by three new sets of rules: goods must clear customs, money must clear currencies, and people must clear cultures. This branch answers four linked questions — why nations trade at all, how border prices distort that trade, in what posture a firm enters someone else’s market, and how to work alongside another culture once you are inside.

1Sub-branch Map

International business studies value creation and exchange across national borders. It is not a brand-new discipline; it is the economics, strategy, and operations you already know, viewed through the filter of the border itself: Lessons 1–2 watch goods and prices cross the line, Lesson 3 watches firms and organizations cross it, and Lesson 4 watches people and ideas cross it. 2Learning Path: Why This Order

From Goods to Money, from Money to Organizations, from Organizations to People

Gains from trade → Border frictions (tariffs · exchange rates) → Entry modes → Cross-cultural management

First establish why go abroad at all — comparative advantage proves trade makes both sides better off, the first principle of all international business. Then look at the price of the border — tariffs and exchange rates are the two levers politicians reach for most often, and you must be able to do their arithmetic. Only once you know the gains and the frictions does the question become how to get in — the entry-mode ladder is a trade-off between risk and control. Finally, once you are in — the organization lands inside another culture, and Hofstede’s dimensions hand you an operational lens. The four lessons climb in one straight line: goods → money → organization → people.

How to study Each lesson takes 30–45 minutes: play with the interactive simulator first to build intuition, then read the main text to upgrade that intuition into concepts, and finish with the quiz as retrieval practice. Neither the Lesson 3 selector nor the Lesson 4 radar chart has a single “correct answer” — the point is to turn the three-axis trade-off and the five dimensions into frameworks you can use to judge real businesses.

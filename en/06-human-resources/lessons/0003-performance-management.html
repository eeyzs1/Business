Human Resources · Lesson 3

Performance Management: KPIs and OKRs

Misaligned goals are an organization’s most expensive hidden cost: a hundred people all working hard, each pointed ten degrees off. This lesson lays out the complete performance-management cycle, takes apart the two mainstream toolsets — appraisal-style KPIs and alignment-style OKRs — and adds the first operation in writing a qualified goal: SMART.

1Performance Management Is a Cycle, Not a Score

The most common misunderstanding is to equate performance management with “year-end scoring and ranking.” In reality, the score is only one step in a cycle: goal setting → coaching → appraisal → feedback — four steps, and then the next round begins. If the appraisal’s output is not “how to improve next,” the cycle is broken — that is not performance management, only performance appraisal.

Concept: Performance Management

The management system that keeps individual effort aligned with organizational direction — and keeps people getting stronger — through a continuous cycle of setting goals, coaching the process, appraising results, and giving feedback. It manages performance, not “people”; its endpoint is improvement, not a score.

2SMART: Write One Qualified Goal First

KPI or OKR, if the very first goal is badly written, everything after it is wasted. The test is the five SMART questions: Specific (say exactly what will be done), Measurable (numbers and indicators), Achievable (a stretch that can actually be reached), Relevant (in service of a bigger goal), Time-bound (a deadline). 3KPI and OKR: Appraisal-Style versus Alignment-Style

Once the goal qualifies, which toolset carries it? The definitions first. A KPI (key performance indicator) cascades top-down from strategy to each role, stresses “committed means delivered,” and is often tied directly to appraisal and raises; it suits businesses with clear outcomes and proven paths — yield, error rates, and retention are natural KPIs. An OKR (objectives and key results) pairs one ambitious objective with several measurable key results and stresses openness and alignment up and down the organization; targets are usually set aggressively — “hitting seventy percent already counts as excellent” is a common saying (indicative) — so most practitioners do not mechanically tie OKRs to raises; otherwise nobody dares to declare an ambitious target. Its lineage traces back to Drucker’s management by objectives (MBO), passed from Intel to Google, where it flourished (indicative).

How to choose in practice

It is not either-or: guard the financial baseline and the mature processes with KPIs, and align innovation directions and cross-department efforts with OKRs — many companies run both in parallel: KPIs run the appraisal, OKRs run the direction.

4Continuous Feedback versus the Annual Review

The annual review has two built-in defects. First, feedback arrives eleven months late — the mistake has long since set, and the best window for correction has closed. Second, memory’s recency effect: at year-end scoring, “the last month or two” crowds out the shape of the whole year. That is why modern practice shifts the weight toward continuous feedback: frequent, short, and improvement-facing — weekly or biweekly one-on-ones (1:1s), immediate recognition and course-correction, quarterly summaries; the annual appraisal shrinks to “a checkpoint summary plus calibration.” Calibration means multiple managers cross-align their scoring scales, preventing strict-versus-lenient incomparability across departments.

One practical rule

Feedback’s half-life is measured in days: the earlier you talk after the event, the greater the leverage for improvement; delay it to year-end and only emotion remains, with no information.

5Case Study: OKRs at Google and ByteDance

Two companies’ experiments in alignment

Google (illustrative): OKRs were brought into the still-young company around 1999 by the investor John Doerr, and the practice later became an all-hands mechanism: everyone’s OKRs are searchable on the intranet, and anyone can see a colleague’s goals for the quarter; setting “uncomfortable” stretch goals is encouraged, and OKRs are generally not mechanically tied to performance ratings — tie them, and nobody dares write an ambitious target down on paper. ByteDance (illustrative, based on commonly reported practice): refreshes OKRs on a two-month rhythm, with goals and progress written into shared documents, transparent to everyone — paired with the “Context, not Control” philosophy, it lets thousands of people align direction quickly; transparency itself is a management instrument.

The lesson: the value of OKRs is not in the spreadsheet but in the word “transparent”: when everyone can see everyone else’s goals, alignment shifts from “cascading messages” to “looking it up yourself” — and keeping OKRs untied from raises protects precisely the courage to set high targets.

Key Terms

Performance Management
The continuous cycle of setting goals, coaching the process, appraising results, and giving feedback — aimed at alignment and improvement.
Key Performance Indicator (KPI)
A steady-state indicator cascaded top-down, usually tied to appraisal.
Objectives and Key Results (OKR)
Ambitious objectives plus measurable milestones, stressing transparency and alignment.
SMART
The five checks of a qualified goal: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.
Key Result
The measurable yardstick inside an OKR that tests whether the objective was achieved.
Continuous Feedback
Frequent, short, improvement-facing everyday feedback that replaces the late annual verdict.
Recency Effect
The memory bias in appraisal where the most recent performance crowds out the whole period.
Calibration
Multiple managers cross-align their scoring scales to remove inconsistency in strictness.
Key Takeaways

Performance management is the cycle “goals → coaching → appraisal → feedback,” not a year-end score sheet. KPIs guard mature businesses through appraisal; OKRs align exploratory ones through transparency — the latter usually untied from raises. Write goals SMART before arguing about tools; the sooner the feedback, the greater the leverage.

The Essence of Marketing: Creating and Keeping Customers

The same bottled water sells for ¥2 at a convenience store and ¥8 at a mountaintop tourist spot — why do buyers appear at both prices? And why do some products blanket the airwaves with advertising yet persuade no one to pay? This lesson sets the tone for the entire Marketing branch: marketing is not a flattering word for selling; it is a system built around creating value for customers — and, because of that, keeping them.

1It All Starts with the Exchange

The management scholar Peter Drucker made a famous claim: the purpose of a business is to create a customer. However impressive your production efficiency, however low your unit cost, if customers will not hand money over in exchange, revenue is zero. Marketing is the complete homework a company does around that exchange: discover needs, design value, communicate value, deliver value. It earns revenue, not renown.

So there is exactly one test for every marketing action: does it make more customers more willing — and more lasting — in exchanging with you? Everything that follows — STP, the 4 Ps, the funnel — is that sentence unpacked.

2Needs, Wants, and Demand: Three Steps

Concept card · The three steps

Needs: the sense of lack human beings are born with — thirst, safety, belonging, esteem; it does not disappear with the times or with any product. Wants: the shape a need takes once it lands on a specific culture and person — for thirst, one person wants an ice-cold cola, another a zero-sugar sparkling water, another a hand-poured coffee. Demand: wants plus purchasing power and action — only the willingness to pay for a specific product constitutes real demand in the market.

This distinction yields marketing’s first discipline: needs can only be recognized, wants can be steered, and demand must be earned with value. Customers can rarely name their need; they can only describe solutions they have seen — which is the deeper point of the “faster horse” story in the case below.

3Customer Value: One Subtraction

Customer Perceived Value = Perceived Benefits − Perceived Costs

Every customer runs this subtraction at speed before reaching for a wallet. Perceived benefits flow mainly from three springs: functional benefits (it solves the problem), emotional benefits (it feels good), and social benefits (identity, belonging, something to talk about). Perceived costs go beyond price: on top of the money there is time cost (queuing, learning to use it) and effort-and-risk cost (choosing wrong, privacy, being talked about). The mountaintop water sells because in the “thirsty right now” situation the time benefit is magnified and the alternatives drop to zero — the subtraction still lands positive.

Notice the word “perceived”: value is computed subjectively by the customer, not by the engineer. Same product, a new context or a new comparison, and the score changes. Half of marketing is managing the two inputs of this subjective subtraction.

4Marketing Myopia: Watch the Need, Not the Product

Concept card · Marketing Myopia

Theodore Levitt’s 1960 Harvard Business Review argument: companies often believe they are in “a certain product business” when they are actually in “the business of satisfying customer needs”. The railroads thought customers needed railroads; customers needed transportation, and the traffic left for cars and airlines. The film giants thought customers needed film; customers needed to keep their images. A company that defines itself by its product goes collectively blind the moment the need is met in a new way.

On this foundation Philip Kotler defined marketing as the process of “creating customers”: identify needs first, then design, communicate, and deliver value. A product is only “today’s best solution” to the need — it is not the business itself.

Case · Ford’s “faster horse” and Apple’s pocket internet

Henry Ford is said to have quipped that if you asked customers what they wanted, they would answer “a faster horse”. The quote may be apocryphal, but the logic holds: customers describe wants (assembled from solutions they have already seen), while the innovator must answer the need underneath — getting there faster, with less effort. Improving the “horse” is product thinking; redesigning what “getting there” means is marketing thinking.

When Apple launched the iPhone in 2007, it did not ask “how do we build a better keyboard phone”. It went back to the need — computing that travels with you, the internet in your pocket — and redefined the phone around a multi-touch screen. First-year sales ran to several million units (illustrative magnitude), and the entire industry was soon forced to rebuild its products to Apple’s definition.

The lesson: Both companies designed for the need, not for the existing product. A product is only the current best answer to a need; answers get replaced, needs endure — the exact counter-example to marketing myopia.

Key Terms

Needs
The sense of lack humans are born with; few in number and impossible to invent.
Wants
The specific forms needs take across cultures and individuals; endlessly many and steerable.
Demand
Wants plus purchasing power and the act of buying; the basis of market transactions.
Customer Perceived Value
The customer’s subjective arithmetic: perceived benefits − perceived costs.
Perceived Costs
The full price a customer pays: money, time, effort, and risk.
Marketing Myopia
Levitt’s term for the strategic blindness caused by defining your industry by product rather than by customer need.
Customer Creation
Kotler’s definition of marketing: the full chain of discovering needs, then designing, communicating, and delivering value.
Key Takeaways

The essence of marketing is creating and keeping customers: identify needs first, then use one subtraction (perceived benefits − perceived costs) to manufacture the customer’s feeling of a “good deal”. Fixate on the product instead of the need, and marketing myopia has already set in.
